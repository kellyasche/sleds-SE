Multivariate Confirmation Analysis: Logistic Regression

The purpose of this project is to explain which characteristics are associated with entry into distinct workforce participation pathways following high school graduation in Southeast Minnesota (EDR 9 and 10). These pathways include sustained employment in the region, sustained employment elsewhere in Minnesota, non-sustained employment, and having no Minnesota employment record.

The policy question of greatest interest is what factors are associated with sustained employment in the region. Earlier analysis relied on bivariate analyses (cross-tabulations, Cramér’s V, and ANOVA) to identify patterns of stratification across workforce participation outcomes. A consistent finding was that postsecondary experiences—particularly credential attainment and where education was completed—are strongly associated with long-term workforce attachment. Academic preparation, early college exposure (PSEO), career and technical education (CTE), and structural demographic factors also demonstrated meaningful relationships.

To strengthen confidence in these findings, a limited multivariate analysis is conducted using logistic regression. The purpose of this modeling is not to establish causality or build a predictive tool, but to examine whether the strongest bivariate relationships remain directionally consistent when key variables are considered simultaneously. This approach helps clarify whether preparation variables retain independent associations after accounting for credential attainment, and whether regional postsecondary completion remains influential when controlling for background and readiness.

Logistic regression is used here as a confirmatory tool rather than the primary analytic framework.


Methodology

Dependent Variables

This analysis models five distinct outcomes, each restricted to the appropriate subset of the cohort:

  1. Sustained regional workforce attachment (grad.year.5) — binary (1 = meaningful employment within the region five years after high school graduation, 0 = otherwise), modeled across the full graduating cohort.
  2. Sustained regional workforce attachment (grad.year.5, college completers only) - binary (1 = meaningful employment within the region five years after high school graduation, 0 = otherwise), modeled across college completers only.
  3. Postsecondary completion (ps.grad) — binary (1 = completed a postsecondary credential, 0 = did not), modeled across all high school graduates, regardless of whether they ever attended postsecondary education.
  4. Postsecondary completion within the region — binary (1 = completed a postsecondary credential within the region, 0 = completed elsewhere in Minnesota or out of state), restricted to postsecondary completers only.
  5. Sub-baccalaureate credential attainment — binary (1 = highest credential earned is an associate degree or less-than-associate credential, 0 = bachelor’s degree or higher), restricted to postsecondary completers only.

Sample

All five models are restricted to the 2011–2019 graduating cohorts for two reasons.

  1. Earlier cohorts (2008–2010) are excluded because PSEO participation was not consistently tracked in the data system prior to 2011 — missingness in that variable is concentrated almost entirely in those years rather than distributed randomly, and including them would risk misclassifying genuine PSEO participants as non-participants. And PSEO participation was identified as having a very strong relationship to our dependent variables in the bivariate analysis.
  2. RaceEthnicity has significant amounts of “Unknown” beginning in 2020.

Independent Variables

Variables were selected based on (1) a demonstrated bivariate relationship with the relevant outcome(s) in the cross-tabulation and ANOVA analysis, and (2) practical usability in a multivariate model, with closely related variables consolidated to avoid multicollinearity. The same variable set and coding is used across all four outcomes.

Notes on Variable Selection

Academic achievement (MCA). MCA Math, Reading, and Science scores are highly correlated with one another (r = 0.63–0.75) and with ACT performance, and all three are excluded from this analysis. MCA Math’s missingness rate differs sharply by English learner status (26.1% vs. 2.4% for other students), and this pattern cannot be reliably classified as an independent confounder versus a mediator of unequal access, resourcing, or administrative practice across student populations. Controlling for it — whether through the raw score or a missingness indicator — risks statistically absorbing a real disparity under the guise of a data-quality correction. No MCA subject is retained as a substitute for another; the concern applies to the construct as a whole, not to Math specifically.

ACT participation and score. Students who did not take the ACT have no composite score by definition — this is a structural absence tied entirely to an observed variable (took.ACT), not missing data in the conventional sense. To retain the full sample rather than dropping non-test-takers, ACTCompositeScore is recoded to 0 for non-test-takers and entered into the model alongside took.ACT. With both terms present, took.ACT captures the effect of test participation itself, while ACTCompositeScore captures the achievement gradient specifically among test-takers, without the two effects being confounded. sat.taken is entered separately, capturing a distinct, smaller subgroup of test-takers not fully overlapping with ACT participation. Because took.ACT shows a participation gap by English learner status (41.4% vs. 11.2%) of a similar scale to the MCA missingness gap that led to MCA’s exclusion, any attenuation of the English learner coefficient once took.ACT enters the model should be interpreted with the same caution — as a variable that may partly reflect unequal access rather than a fully independent behavior — rather than treated as the disparity being explained away.

Variables dropped. MCA.M, MCA.R, and MCA.S are excluded all three MCA subjects for the reasons described above.

Modeling Approach

Each outcome is modeled using a sequential block-entry approach, adding one group of predictors at a time to assess the relative contribution of each. Each block is compared to the prior one using a likelihood ratio test to confirm it adds significant explanatory power, with AIC and pseudo-R² tracked across the sequence to summarize overall model improvement.

  • Model 1: Demographics
  • Model 2: + High school characteristics
  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments
  • Model 4: + College completion

Sustained regional workforce attachment - college completers only

  • Model 2: + High school characteristics
  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments
  • Model 4: + highest credential earned
  • Model 5: + post-secondary location
  • Model 6: Dependent on results

Postsecondary completion, all high school graduates:

  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments

Postsecondary completion within the region, completers only:

  • Model 1: Demographics
  • Model 2: + High school characteristics
  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments

Sub-baccalaureate credential attainment, completers only:

  • Model 1: Demographics
  • Model 2: + High school characteristics
  • Model 3: + High school accomplishments

Following these base model sequences, additional modeling will be considered only if the results indicate a need for further specification (e.g., interaction effects, additional mediation checks).


Analysis Approach

This multivariate analysis is structured as a series of theoretically guided logistic regression models designed to evaluate mechanisms associated with sustained regional employment. The goal is confirmatory testing of previously observed patterns rather than prediction or causal inference.


Evaluation Criteria

Results will be reported using: - Odds ratios - 95% confidence intervals - Statistical significance levels - Directional interpretation

Model comparison metrics (AIC and pseudo R²) will be used to assess relative improvement across specifications within each outcome’s model sequence, but emphasis will remain on coefficient stability, attenuation patterns, and substantive interpretation rather than overall fit.

Interpretation Framework

Interpretation will focus on:

  • Direction and relative magnitude of associations
  • Changes in coefficients across blocks within each model sequence
  • Evidence of attenuation as additional blocks are introduced.

Findings will be described as associations holding other included variables constant. No causal claims will be made. Together, these models provide multivariate confirmation of patterns identified in earlier descriptive analyses while preserving conceptual clarity and interpretability.

Overview

Dependent variable: grad.year.5 (binary — meaningful employment within the region five years after high school graduation) Population: Full graduating cohort (2011–2019), n = 47,693 Purpose: Confirmatory multivariate testing of associations identified in earlier bivariate analysis; not causal or predictive.

Headline Findings

  1. CTE participation is the most consistent positive predictor of regional attachment. Both CTE Concentrator/Completor (OR ≈ 1.45) and CTE Participant (OR ≈ 1.29) categories, plus total CTE courses taken (OR ≈ 1.03/course), remain stable and significant from Model 3 through Model 4.

  2. ACT participation and ACT score pull in opposite directions (“two forces” pattern). Taking the ACT at all is strongly associated with higher odds of regional attachment (OR ≈ 3.6–3.9), while higher composite scores among test-takers are associated with lower odds (OR ≈ 0.93–0.94 per point). SAT-taking and AP exam participation follow the same negative-association pattern, consistent with higher academic orientation predicting geographic departure.

  3. Postsecondary completion (ps.grad) is independently associated with higher odds of regional attachment (OR = 1.453) even after controlling for demographics, HS context, and HS accomplishments.

  4. Race/ethnicity gaps persist across all four models with little attenuation. Black and American Indian graduates show the largest and most stable gaps relative to White graduates (OR ≈ 0.44–0.48 in the final model), holding all other variables constant.

  5. Gender fully attenuates to null by Model 3 — an initially significant negative association for female graduates dissolves once HS accomplishments enter, suggesting it was proxying for accomplishment-related variables rather than reflecting an independent gender effect.

  6. economic.status and pseo.participant both attenuate to null by Model 4, once ps.grad is added — suggesting their associations with regional attachment operate primarily through postsecondary completion rather than independently.

Diagnostics & Data Quality Notes

VIF: took.ACT and ACTCompositeScore.filled show elevated VIFs (≈9.8–11.8) from Model 3 onward — expected and structural, given ACTCompositeScore.filled is coded 0 for non-test-takers by design. Their individual coefficients should be interpreted jointly rather than independently. All other variables remain well within acceptable VIF range (< 2) throughout.

sat.taken subgroup size: n = 1,082 (2.3% of cohort). CI (0.439–0.631) is reasonably precise despite the small subgroup, so direction/magnitude are treated as reliable, with the exact point estimate flagged as somewhat less stable than larger-subgroup estimates.

Dem_Desc/edr: Initially flagged as a possible collinearity issue based on a note carried over from prior methodology; confirmed not applicable to this data (no aliasing, low VIF for both) and corrected in interpretation.

economic.status sign flip: Direction reversed from positive (Models 1–2) to negative (Model 3) before attenuating to null (Model 4) — reflects a real shift in the model, not a coding error, and traces to postsecondary completion as the likely mediating pathway.

Taking the ACT at all is strongly associated with higher odds of regional attachment (OR ≈ 3.6–3.9), while higher composite scores among test-takers are associated with lower odds (OR ≈ 0.93–0.94 per point). SAT-taking and AP exam participation follow the same negative-association pattern, consistent with higher academic orientation predicting geographic departure.

  3. Postsecondary completion (ps.grad) is independently associated with higher odds of regional attachment (OR = 1.453) even after controlling for demographics, HS context, and HS accomplishments.

  4. Race/ethnicity gaps persist across all four models with little attenuation. Black and American Indian graduates show the largest and most stable gaps relative to White graduates (OR ≈ 0.44–0.48 in the final model), holding all other variables constant.

  5. Gender fully attenuates to null by Model 3 — an initially significant negative association for female graduates dissolves once HS accomplishments enter, suggesting it was proxying for accomplishment-related variables rather than reflecting an independent gender effect.

  6. economic.status and pseo.participant both attenuate to null by Model 4, once ps.grad is added — suggesting their associations with regional attachment operate primarily through postsecondary completion rather than independently.

Diagnostics & Data Quality Notes

  • sat.taken subgroup size: n = 1,082 (2.3% of cohort). CI (0.439–0.631) is reasonably precise despite the small subgroup, so direction/magnitude are treated as reliable, with the exact point estimate flagged as somewhat less stable than larger-subgroup estimates.
  • economic.status sign flip: Direction reversed from positive (Models 1–2) to negative (Model 3) before attenuating to null (Model 4) — reflects a real shift in the model, not a coding error, and traces to postsecondary completion as the likely mediating pathway.

DV2 Summary: What Predicts Sustained Regional Workforce Attachment (College Completers Only)

Overview

Dependent variable: grad.year.5 (binary — meaningful employment within the region five years after high school graduation) Population: College completers only (2011–2019 graduating cohorts), n = 22,128 Purpose: Confirmatory multivariate testing of associations identified in earlier bivariate analysis; not causal or predictive.

Model Sequence

Model 1 (Demographics): AIC 28,631 | Pseudo-R² 0.0045 | — (baseline) Model 2 (+ HS characteristics): AIC 28,617 | Pseudo-R² 0.0054 | χ²(5) = 24.13, p < 0.001 Model 3 (+ HS accomplishments): AIC 27,301 | Pseudo-R² 0.0517 | χ²(8) = 1,331.95, p < 0.001 Model 4 (+ Highest credential earned): AIC 27,133 | Pseudo-R² 0.0576 | χ²(1) = 169.73, p < 0.001 Model 5 (+ Postsecondary location): AIC 25,727 | Pseudo-R² 0.1066 | χ²(1) = 1,408.37, p < 0.001

Each block added statistically significant explanatory power. The largest gain by far came from Model 5 (postsecondary location) — both in deviance reduction and pseudo-R² improvement, exceeding even the HS-accomplishments block.

Variables Retained (Final Model 5)

Demographics: Gender, RaceEthnicity, economic.status, SpecialEdStatus, english.learner HS characteristics: Dem_Desc, edr, avg.wages.pct.state, avg.unemp.rate HS accomplishments: pseo.participant, took.ACT, ACTCompositeScore.filled, ap.exam, sat.taken, cte.achievement, total.cte.courses.taken Credential attainment: highest.cred.level, ps.grad.location

No variables were dropped across the DV2 sequence. No Model 6 was specified.

Headline Findings

  1. Postsecondary location is the strongest predictor in the DV2 sequence. Completers who earned their credential outside the region show 69.0% lower odds of sustained regional attachment (OR = 0.310) compared to those who completed within the region, holding all else constant.

  2. CTE participation remains a consistent positive predictor, as in DV1. Both CTE Concentrator/Completor (OR ≈ 1.37–1.46 across blocks) and CTE Participant (OR ≈ 1.22–1.29) categories, plus total CTE courses taken, remain stable and significant from Model 3 onward.

  3. ACT participation and ACT score continue to pull in opposite directions. Taking the ACT is strongly positive (OR ≈ 3.1–4.7 depending on block), while higher composite scores among test-takers trend negative (OR ≈ 0.92–0.94 per point). SAT-taking and AP exam participation follow the same negative pattern, consistent with the DV1 findings.

  4. Highest credential level’s effect attenuates substantially once postsecondary location enters. The bachelor’s-vs-sub-bachelor’s gap narrows from OR = 0.631 (Model 4) to OR = 0.808 (Model 5) — a meaningful share of the credential-level association appears to run through where the credential was earned rather than credential level itself.

  5. Several demographic effects reverse direction relative to DV1. SpecialEdStatus and english.learner are both positive in DV2’s early models (Models 1–2) before shifting toward null or negative by later blocks, in contrast to DV1 where SpecialEdStatus was consistently negative throughout. economic.status is positive through Model 3 before attenuating to null once highest.cred.level and ps.grad.location enter.

  6. Race/ethnicity gaps persist but with more instability than DV1. Black and American Indian completers show the largest gaps relative to White completers, though RaceEthnicityAI’s significance is marginal by Model 5 (p = 0.053) and RaceEthnicityHispanic is not significant in any DV2 model, unlike DV1 where it was significant throughout.

Diagnostics & Data Quality Notes

VIF: took.ACT and ACTCompositeScore.filled show elevated VIFs (≈6.0–7.3) from Model 3 onward — expected and structural, given ACTCompositeScore.filled is coded 0 for non-test-takers by design. All other variables remain within acceptable VIF range throughout, including highest.cred.level and ps.grad.location, both of which show low VIF despite their outsized coefficient effects.

avg.wages.pct.state shows notable instability across blocks — non-significant in Model 2, significant and positive from Model 3 onward, and growing substantially larger by Model 5 (OR = 3.342) — this movement is larger than typical attenuation/strengthening patterns seen elsewhere in either DV sequence and may warrant a closer look before being treated as a stable finding.

RaceEthnicityAI carries wide confidence intervals throughout the DV2 sequence, consistent with the smaller subgroup size within the college-completer population relative to the full DV1 cohort.

Open Items

Model 6 was not specified — methodology held it open as “dependent on results,” and it was decided not to pursue further specification. No further specification (interactions, mediation checks) has been run yet.

Variables Retained (Final Model 3)

Demographics: Gender, RaceEthnicity, economic.status, SpecialEdStatus, english.learner HS characteristics: Dem_Desc, edr, avg.wages.pct.state, avg.unemp.rate HS accomplishments: pseo.participant, took.ACT, ACTCompositeScore.filled, ap.exam, sat.taken, cte.achievement, total.cte.courses.taken

No variables were dropped across the DV3 sequence.

Headline Findings

  1. Demographics alone explain far more variance in postsecondary completion than in regional attachment. Model 1’s pseudo-R² (0.1146) is roughly 20x larger than DV1’s or DV2’s equivalent Model 1, and the HS-accomplishments block (Model 3) produced the largest single jump in pseudo-R² seen across all three DV sequences (0.115 → 0.218).

  2. Academic-orientation variables run in the opposite direction from DV1/DV2. Taking the ACT, higher ACT composite scores, AP exam participation, and SAT-taking are all positively associated with postsecondary completion here (OR range ≈ 1.09–1.82), whereas the same variables were negatively associated with regional attachment in DV1 and DV2. This is the clearest structural contrast across the three outcomes: academic orientation appears to pull toward postsecondary completion but away from regional workforce attachment.

  3. Economic disadvantage and special education status remain the strongest negative predictors throughout. Both attenuate somewhat once HS accomplishments are added (economic.status: OR 0.333 → 0.477; SpecialEdStatus: OR 0.212 → 0.467), but both remain large, significant, negative effects in the final model.

  4. Gender is a large, stable, positive predictor across all three models. Female graduates consistently show ~58–75% higher odds of postsecondary completion than male graduates, holding other variables constant — the largest and most stable demographic effect in the DV3 sequence.

  5. PSEO participation is a strong positive predictor (OR = 1.781 in Model 3) — one of the larger accomplishment-block effects in this sequence.

  6. CTE effects are present but smaller in magnitude than in DV1/DV2. CTE Concentrator/Completor and Participant categories remain significant and positive, but with OR ≈ 1.06–1.10, compared to the 1.2–1.5 range seen in DV1/DV2. total.cte.courses.taken loses significance entirely in Model 3 — the only DV sequence where this term does not hold up.

  7. Race/ethnicity patterns shift substantially across blocks. RaceEthnicityAsian/PI attenuates fully to null by Model 3 (from a significant positive effect in Models 1–2), and RaceEthnicityBlack’s gap narrows considerably, while RaceEthnicityAI and RaceEthnicityHispanic remain significant and negative throughout.

  8. english.learner reverses sign once HS accomplishments enter (Model 2 → Model 3: OR 0.86 → 1.22) — a sign flip rather than simple attenuation, consistent with the pattern of EL effects shifting once participation-related variables are added, as seen in DV1/DV2.

DV4 Summary: What Predicts Postsecondary Completion Within the Region (College Completers Only)

Overview

Dependent variable: ps.grad.location (binary — completed postsecondary credential within the region, coded 1 = within region, 0 = elsewhere in Minnesota or out of state) Population: Postsecondary completers only (2011–2019 graduating cohorts), n = 33,816 Purpose: Confirmatory multivariate testing of associations identified in earlier bivariate analysis; not causal or predictive.

Model Sequence

Model 1 (Demographics): AIC 44,647 | Pseudo-R² 0.0167 | — (baseline) Model 2 (+ HS characteristics): AIC 44,405 | Pseudo-R² 0.0223 | χ²(5) = 251.48, p < 0.001 Model 3 (+ HS accomplishments): AIC 42,139 | Pseudo-R² 0.0725 | χ²(8) = 2,282.49, p < 0.001

This completes the DV4 model sequence per the methodology (Demographics → HS characteristics → HS accomplishments, with no further blocks specified for this outcome).

Variables Retained (Final Model 3)

Demographics: Gender, RaceEthnicity, economic.status, SpecialEdStatus, english.learner HS characteristics: Dem_Desc, edr, avg.wages.pct.state, avg.unemp.rate HS accomplishments: pseo.participant, took.ACT, ACTCompositeScore.filled, ap.exam, sat.taken, cte.achievement, total.cte.courses.taken

No variables were dropped across the DV4 sequence.

Headline Findings

  1. ACT participation is the strongest predictor in the DV4 sequence. Taking the ACT is associated with 6.4x higher odds of completing postsecondary education within the region (OR = 6.384) — the largest took.ACT coefficient observed across any of the four DV sequences to date.

  2. The “two forces” pattern holds clearly here, mirroring DV1/DV2. ACT participation is strongly positive, while ACT composite score, AP exam participation, and SAT-taking are all negatively associated with completing within the region — consistent with higher academic orientation predicting departure from the region for postsecondary study, not just for post-graduation employment.

  3. avg.wages.pct.state shows a large, stable negative effect. Higher local wages relative to the state average are associated with substantially lower odds of completing within the region (OR ≈ 0.10–0.11), and this effect holds steady across Models 2 and 3 rather than shifting — unlike the instability this variable showed in the DV2 sequence.

  4. Urbanicity (Dem_Desc) is a strong, stable positive predictor. Both “Entirely urban” (OR = 1.802) and “Urban/town/rural mix” (OR = 1.248) categories show meaningfully higher odds of completing within the region relative to the reference category, strengthening from Model 2 to Model 3.

  5. SpecialEdStatus and english.learner both attenuate substantially toward null once HS accomplishments are added. SpecialEdStatus goes from a significant positive effect in Models 1–2 (OR ≈ 1.54–1.55) to non-significant (OR = 1.046) in Model 3; english.learner attenuates from significant (OR = 1.632) to marginal (OR = 1.139, p = 0.066) — suggesting these early demographic associations run largely through HS accomplishment/participation variables.

  6. economic.status remains positive and significant throughout, though it attenuates somewhat once HS accomplishments enter (OR = 1.714 → 1.699 → 1.390 across the three models) — economically disadvantaged completers consistently show higher odds of completing within the region.

  7. Race/ethnicity patterns are more mixed than in other DV sequences. RaceEthnicityHispanic is the only category showing consistently higher odds relative to White completers (OR ≈ 1.34–1.35 throughout); RaceEthnicityAI remains significant and negative; RaceEthnicityBlack is non-significant in all three models; RaceEthnicityAsian/PI attenuates fully to null by Model 3.

Diagnostics & Data Quality Notes

VIF: took.ACT and ACTCompositeScore.filled show elevated VIFs (≈5.5–6.4) in Model 3 — expected and structural, consistent with the pattern in DV1/DV2/DV3. All other variables remain within acceptable VIF range throughout.

edrEDR 10 - Southeast does not reach significance in any DV4 model, consistent with the pattern seen in DV3 — region alone shows no independent association with either postsecondary completion or completion location once other variables are in the model.

total.cte.courses.taken and pseo.participant are both non-significant in the final model — the CTE courses variable has now failed to reach significance in DV3 and DV4, while holding up in DV1 and DV2.

Executive Summary: Multivariate Confirmation Analysis of Workforce Participation Pathways Southeast Minnesota (EDR 9 and 10)

Purpose and Approach

This analysis used sequential block-entry logistic regression to test whether the strongest relationships identified in earlier bivariate analysis — particularly the role of postsecondary credential attainment, credential level, and location of completion — remain directionally consistent once considered simultaneously with demographic, high school context, and accomplishment variables. Five outcomes were modeled across the 2011–2019 graduating cohorts from Southeast Minnesota (EDR 9 and 10): sustained regional workforce attachment (both for the full cohort and for college completers only), postsecondary completion, completion location, and credential level. Consistent with the confirmatory (not causal, not predictive) framing of this project, findings are described throughout as associations holding other variables constant, not as causal effects.

The Central Story: Two Divergent Pathways

A college-preparatory, mobility-oriented pathway — marked by higher ACT composite scores (conditional on taking the test), AP exam participation, and SAT participation — consistently predicts completing a postsecondary credential (DV3), completing a bachelor’s degree rather than an associate’s or less (DV5), and completing that credential outside the region (DV4). Among people who complete a credential, this same pathway predicts lower odds of sustained regional employment (DV1/DV2). These variables mark students headed toward more advanced credentials and greater geographic mobility, with regional workforce attachment as the trade-off.

A CTE-anchored, regionally-oriented pathway runs largely in the opposite direction. CTE engagement (achievement level and course count) showed a modest but real positive relationship with completing a postsecondary credential at all (DV3) — smaller in magnitude than most other accomplishment effects, but statistically significant, not absent. It consistently and more strongly predicted completing that credential locally rather than out-of-region (DV4), and predicted sustained regional employment among both the full cohort and completers (DV1/DV2). For credential level (DV5), CTE engagement (particularly total courses taken, its strongest effect of any outcome tested) predicted sub-baccalaureate rather than bachelor’s attainment — placing CTE consistently on the regionally-anchored, sub-baccalaureate side of every outcome where it appears.

PSEO participation tells a distinct story from CTE rather than a parallel one. It was the single strongest predictor of postsecondary completion found anywhere in the project (nearly double the odds, DV3), and it predicted bachelor’s completion over sub-baccalaureate attainment in DV5 — aligning it with the mobility-track variables on that outcome. But PSEO showed no reliable independent relationship with regional employment attachment once other accomplishment variables were controlled (DV1’s Model 3 effect fully attenuated to null once postsecondary completion itself entered; DV2 and DV4 showed no significant PSEO effect at any stage). In this data, PSEO functions primarily as a general attainment accelerant leaning toward bachelor’s completion, rather than as a regionally-anchoring variable in its own right.

A recurring ACT-specific nuance: took.ACT and ACT composite score point in opposite directions once combined in every model where both appear. Taking the test at all is associated with one direction, but each additional point pulls back the other way — meaning a low scorer who took the test can look more “regionally anchored” or more likely to complete than someone who never tested, while a high scorer looks considerably more academically oriented and mobile. This threshold effect should be described explicitly rather than reported as a single flat “ACT participation” finding.

The Most Important Unresolved Finding: The Black-White Gap

Across three of the five outcomes — sustained regional workforce attachment for the full cohort (DV1), for completers only (DV2), and sub-baccalaureate credential attainment (DV5) — the disparity between Black and White graduates did not narrow once accomplishment variables (PSEO, ACT, AP, SAT, CTE) were controlled. In DV1 and DV2 the gap remained essentially stable across all model blocks; in DV5 it widened once accomplishments were added (OR moving from 0.763 to 0.573 between Models 2 and 3). No accomplishment variable, credential level, or completion location tested anywhere in this project explains this gap.

This same disparity behaved differently on the other two outcomes: it narrowed substantially once accomplishments were controlled for whether someone completes a postsecondary credential at all (DV3, OR moving from roughly 0.71 to 0.88), and it was non-significant at every stage for completion location (DV4). Taken together, this suggests Black graduates’ rates of completing a credential are reasonably well explained by measured differences in accomplishment and preparation access — but a real, unexplained disparity remains specifically in long-term regional employment attachment and in credential level, independent of everything else tested. This is the most important equity finding to lead with in any final report, precisely because it is not explained by the very access-oriented variables that explain most other disparities in this project.

American Indian graduates showed a related but distinct pattern: a persistent, largely stable negative gap across DV1, DV2, DV3, and DV4, but no significant relationship at all with credential level (DV5) — suggesting the AI disparity operates on whether and where people work and complete credentials, but not on which credential level they reach.

Demographic Disadvantage: Two Different Underlying Mechanisms

Economic status, SpecialEdStatus, and English learner status showed two distinct structural patterns depending on the outcome:

A suppression pattern, seen in DV1 (full-cohort employment attachment): economic.status was null-to-positive in the demographics-only baseline, turned significantly negative once HS accomplishments were added, and then attenuated fully to null once postsecondary completion itself entered the model. This indicates the early, more optimistic reading was an artifact of omitted variables rather than the true underlying relationship.

An access pattern, seen in DV3 and DV5 (completion and credential level): economic.status and SpecialEdStatus both showed clear disadvantage immediately in the demographics-only baseline, and that disadvantage shrank substantially — without fully disappearing — once accomplishment variables entered. This is consistent with a real share of each gap running through unequal access to PSEO, ACT, AP, and CTE opportunities. SpecialEdStatus showed the single largest demographic effect size found anywhere in the project (DV5 Model 1: more than four times the odds of sub-baccalaureate attainment), attenuating to roughly 1.8x by the final model.

English learner status followed a more outcome-dependent pattern than in a typical access story: it attenuated to full non-significance in DV5 (a clean access-pattern result) and reversed sign entirely in DV3 (negative in the demographics-only model, positive once accomplishments were controlled). It also showed positive associations in DV2 and DV4 at the demographics stage that attenuated toward null as accomplishments entered — the opposite starting point from the typical “early disadvantage” pattern, worth treating as its own distinct finding rather than folding into either mechanism above.

Gender: Different Stories on Different Outcomes

Female graduates were consistently more likely to complete a postsecondary credential (DV3) and, to an attenuated but still significant degree, more likely to complete a bachelor’s rather than a sub-baccalaureate credential (DV5). For regional employment attachment, gender behaved differently depending on the population: among the full cohort (DV1), an early negative association for women fully attenuated to null once HS accomplishments were controlled. Among completers specifically (DV2), gender moved in the opposite direction — a null association in the demographics-only model became a significant positive association for women once HS accomplishments entered, and this held through the final model. These are not contradictory findings; they reflect different mechanisms across different populations and outcomes, and should be reported as distinct results rather than reconciled into one general “gender effect.”

The Single Strongest Lever Identified: Location of Postsecondary Completion

Among every variable tested across all five outcomes, location of postsecondary completion (DV2, Model 5) produced the largest effect size and the largest single-block improvement in model fit found anywhere in the project. Completing a credential outside the region was associated with roughly a 69% reduction in the odds of sustained regional employment — exceeding the effect of credential level itself (roughly a 19–37% reduction for bachelor’s vs. sub-baccalaureate, depending on model specification), though both were independently significant and not fully redundant with one another. If this project’s findings point to one clear, actionable lever for regional workforce policy, it is this one: where people complete their postsecondary education matters more than almost anything else tested for whether they end up working in the region afterward.

Regional Geography (EDR): Consistently Present, Modestly Shaped by Outcome

EDR 10 (relative to EDR 9) showed a stable, significant positive association with regional employment attachment across DV1 and DV2, and a significant negative association with sub-baccalaureate attainment in DV5 (i.e., EDR 10 graduates were more likely to complete a bachelor’s than an associate’s or less). It was not a significant predictor of postsecondary completion (DV3) or completion location (DV4) at any stage — the only two outcomes where region showed no independent association once other variables were included.

Outstanding Technical Item

avg.wages.pct.state reached significance in most models across four of the five DVs (DV1 marginal only, DV2/DV3/DV4 significant, DV5 never significant), but its odds ratio is not directly interpretable as computed — it reflects a full 1-unit (100 percentage point) change on a variable that realistically ranges only about 0.7–1.0. This should be rescaled (e.g., per 0.10 increment or per standard deviation) before any odds ratio involving this variable appears in a final report table. The direction and significance of the findings involving this variable are sound; only the magnitude as currently expressed needs correction. It’s also worth noting this variable showed unusual instability across blocks in DV2 specifically (non-significant → significant → growing substantially larger), which may warrant a closer look before being treated as a fully stable finding there.

Bottom Line

Two coherent, largely opposing pathways run through this analysis: a college-preparatory track associated with completing more advanced credentials and greater geographic mobility, and a CTE-anchored track associated with regional employment and regional completion, with PSEO functioning as a distinct attainment-accelerant that leans toward the mobility track rather than fitting neatly into either pathway. Layered on top of this structural finding are two distinct patterns of demographic disparity — one where disadvantage is suppressed until accomplishments are controlled, another where disadvantage is visible early and partly explained by unequal access to those same accomplishments — and, cutting across three of the five outcomes examined, a Black-White gap in long-term employment attachment and credential level that neither pattern explains. Of every lever tested, location of postsecondary completion stands out as the one with by far the largest, most policy-relevant effect on whether graduates remain in the regional workforce.
