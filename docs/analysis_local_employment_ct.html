There are a number of “states” in which an individual in this dataset are assigned;

  1. Has meaningful employment
    1. In the same county as their high school.
    2. In the same EDR as their high school, but not county.
    3. In Minnesota, but not in Southern Minnesota.
  2. Is attending post-secondary.
  3. Has a MN employment record but it’s not meaningful, and not attending post-secondary.
  4. Does not have a MN employment record and is not attending post-secondary.

There are three “times” in which we checked to see if the individual had meaningful employment within these geographies;

  1. one year after graduating high school
  2. five years after graduation, and
  3. ten years after graduation.

Meaningful employment is determined by whether an individual worked 1,000 hours for an employer during time X. In addition, if the individual is working for an employer but not meaningful at time X but worked 1,000 hours for that employer during another year it’s still considered “meaningful”.

Due to time x potentially being passed the date of the latest data (2023) for some individuals, the analysis below will filter out all individuals where time x is after 2023.

Methodology

For this analysis we are keeping it simple. For categorical variables I’ll be using the Cramer’s v-square measure of association. The V^2 value is from 0 to 1 with 0 meaning there’s no association and a value closer to 1 indicates a strong association.

Cramér’s V Value Interpretation
0.00–0.10 Negligible association
0.10–0.20 Weak
0.20–0.40 Moderate
0.40–0.60 Relatively strong
0.60–0.80 Strong
0.80–1.00 Very strong (possibly redundant)

For the variables with continuous values I’ll be using the Wilcoxon two paired groups test. P-value will be used to determine whether we can reject the NULL hypothesis at a .01 level. I’ll also include boxplots to see in which direction there might be influence from the variables.

We are going to split up the analysis into two different datasets. The first dataset will combine 3 of the previous states into one.

  • Has meaningful employment anywhere in Southeast Minnesota, which combines the following states;
    • Meaningful emp County
    • Meaningful emp EDR
    • Meaningful emp region

The remaining states will stay the same.

  • Has meaningful employment in Minnesota, but not in Southern Minnesota
  • Is attending a post-secondary institution.
  • Has a MN employment record but it’s not meaningful, and they are not attending post-secondary.
  • Does not have a MN employment record and is not attending post-secondary.

WORKFORCE PARTICIPATION OUTCOMES — SOUTHEAST MINNESOTA

The chart below provides the percentage of students that fall into each workforce participation category at 1, 5, and 10-years after graduating high school.

At Year 1, the distribution is heavily shaped by postsecondary enrollment. 54.4% are attending postsecondary, 14.0% are in meaningful regional employment, 4.6% in meaningful MN employment, 16.8% in non-meaningful employment, and 10.2% have no MN employment record.

By Year 5, postsecondary attendance has dropped sharply to 18.5% as graduates complete or exit credentials. Meaningful regional employment rises to 27.2% and meaningful MN employment to 16.3%. Non-meaningful employment holds at 17.7% and those with no MN record rise modestly to 20.4%.

By Year 10, postsecondary attendance is essentially zero (n=1). Meaningful regional employment reaches 29.7% and meaningful MN employment 21.1%. However, the most notable feature is the sharp rise in no MN employment record to 33.4%, while non-meaningful employment declines to 15.8%.

The large and growing no-MN-employment-record group at Year 10 is the most important pattern to flag. This likely reflects a combination of out-of-state migration, self-employment, and genuine labor force disconnection — and will be a central interpretive challenge throughout the analysis.

Summary

The demographic variables as a group show moderate associations with workforce outcomes, with effect sizes generally strongest at Year 1 — where postsecondary attendance patterns dominate the distribution — and weakening over time as graduates sort into more stable labor market positions. The three language-related variables show a consistent and notable pattern across all time points: graduates from non-English language backgrounds show elevated meaningful MN employment at Year 1, likely reflecting concentration in specific industries or urban labor markets outside the Southeast region rather than broad workforce advantage.

RANKING BY STRENGTH OF ASSOCIATION (Cramer’s V at Year 5 as primary sort, with Year 1 and Year 10 as tiebreakers)

  1. SpecialEdStatus (V=0.274 / 0.145 / 0.086) Special education status is the strongest demographic predictor at Year 1 and remains the most consistently differentiated variable across all three time points. The postsecondary attendance gap is the largest of any demographic variable, and the non-meaningful employment disadvantage persists through Year 10. Notably, regional employment reaches full parity by Year 10, suggesting that special education graduates who connect to the regional workforce do so successfully — but a larger share remains in non-meaningful or marginal employment throughout the observation window.

  2. economic.status (V=0.260 / 0.121 / 0.083) Economic disadvantage is the second strongest predictor and shows the most complete convergence over time of any variable in this group. The large Year 1 postsecondary gap closes substantially by Year 5, and meaningful regional employment reaches near parity by Year 5 and full parity by Year 10. The most durable disadvantage is in meaningful MN employment, pointing to differential access to college-driven career pathways rather than a broad regional workforce deficit.

  3. english.learner (V=0.164 / 0.078 / 0.059) English learner status is the strongest of the three language variables and the primary language indicator for multivariate modeling. The Year 1 postsecondary gap and elevated no-MN-record rate are the defining features, with gaps persisting — though narrowing — through Year 10. The elevated meaningful MN employment at Year 1 is an analytically interesting anomaly shared across all three language variables.

  4. Gender (V=0.112 / 0.058 / 0.048) Gender shows a moderate Year 1 association driven primarily by differential postsecondary attendance rather than direct workforce sorting. Males enter regional employment earlier; females attend postsecondary at higher rates and enter the labor market later. By Year 10, differences are small and consistent — males retain a modest advantage in both meaningful regional and MN employment, while females show higher non-meaningful employment rates.

  5. non.english.home (V=0.110 / 0.054 / 0.060) Non-english home largely mirrors english.learner with consistently weaker effect sizes. It is retained for descriptive bivariate purposes but excluded from multivariate models. The notable absence of a meaningful regional employment gap at Year 1 — unlike english.learner — suggests the two variables capture partially distinct subpopulations despite their conceptual overlap.

  6. RaceEthnicity (V=0.073 / 0.044 / 0.041) Race and ethnicity shows weak overall associations but contains substantively important within-group variation. The Year 10 no-MN-record rates for Asian/PI (45.1%), American Indian (41.1%), and Black (39.7%) graduates are the most striking findings and likely reflect differential out-of-state migration following postsecondary completion rather than labor force disconnection. Hispanic graduates show a distinctive pattern of relatively strong regional employment at Years 5 and 10 despite lower postsecondary attendance at Year 1. AI findings should be interpreted with caution throughout given small cell sizes.

  7. LimitedEnglishProficiencyIndicator (V=0.079 / 0.032 / 0.031) The weakest demographic predictor and the most rapidly declining in association over time. Summary

The high school characteristics group — comprising community demographic description (Dem_Desc), economic development region (edr), average local unemployment rate (avg.unemp.rate), and average wages as a percent of state average (avg.wages.pct.state) — is collectively the weakest variable group examined so far. Effect sizes are small throughout, and two of the four variables add negligible practical explanatory power despite reaching statistical significance given the large sample size.

RANKING BY STRENGTH OF ASSOCIATION

  1. avg.unemp.rate (F=107.7 / 21.7 / 59.0) The average local unemployment rate is the strongest variable in this group and the only one with substantively meaningful effect sizes. At Year 1, graduates in meaningful regional employment come from lower unemployment areas, while the no-MN-record group comes from the highest unemployment areas — consistent with local labor market conditions shaping early workforce entry. By Year 5 and 10, a clearer long-run mechanism emerges: stronger local labor markets anchor graduates regionally, while higher local unemployment is associated with out-of-region employment or labor force marginality. This variable is retained in multivariate models and represents the most analytically useful signal in this group.

  2. edr (V=0.017 / 0.049 / 0.055) EDR shows weak but growing associations over time — unusual relative to most variables. The two EDRs are essentially indistinguishable at Year 1 but diverge modestly by Years 5 and 10, with EDR 9 graduates showing higher meaningful MN employment and EDR 10 graduates showing higher no-MN-record rates. Regional employment reaches near parity across both EDRs at all time points. The emerging gap is concentrated entirely in out-of-region outcomes and may reflect the geographic pull of the Twin Cities metro for EDR 9 graduates. Despite statistical significance, practical magnitude remains small throughout.

  3. Dem_Desc (V=0.041 / 0.046 / 0.030) Community demographic description shows the weakest and flattest association of any variable in the group. The urban/rural character of a graduate’s high school has virtually no bearing on long-run workforce outcomes in Southeast Minnesota. The only substantively interesting signal is a modestly higher postsecondary attendance rate for entirely urban graduates at Years 1 and 5, consistent with greater access to four-year institutions, but this advantage dissipates entirely by Year 10.

  4. avg.wages.pct.state (F=78.4 / 58.3 / 16.2) Despite reaching statistical significance at all three time points, avg.wages.pct.state produces effect sizes so small — raw mean differences on the order of 0.003 to 0.013 percentage points — as to be analytically meaningless. This confirms the framework decision to exclude it from multivariate models. It is noted here for completeness only.

CROSS-CUTTING THEMES

The high school characteristics group as a whole tells a consistent story: where a graduate went to high school matters very little for long-run workforce outcomes in Southeast Minnesota, with the partial exception of the local unemployment rate, which reflects genuine labor market conditions rather than institutional or geographic sorting. The absence of strong place-based effects in this region contrasts with what might be expected and suggests that individual-level factors — particularly postsecondary pathways and high school accomplishments — are likely to carry much more explanatory weight in the multivariate stages. The two disconnection signatures identified in the demographics group do not map cleanly onto geographic characteristics here, reinforcing the interpretation that disconnection in Southeast Minnesota is driven more by individual circumstances than by place.

Dem_Desc shows weak associations with workforce outcomes across all three time points (V=0.041 at Year 1, V=0.046 at Year 5, V=0.030 at Year 10), and the association is essentially flat over time — unusual relative to most demographic variables which show stronger Year 1 effects driven by postsecondary sorting.

At Year 1, the most notable difference is in postsecondary attendance, where entirely urban graduates attend at a higher rate (57.8%) than urban/town/rural mix (51.9%) or town/rural mix (54.6%) graduates. Entirely urban graduates show the lowest no-MN-record rate (8.8%) and the lowest non-meaningful employment rate (15.5%), while urban/town/rural mix graduates show the highest no-MN-record rate (11.3%).

Overall, community demographic description shows the weakest association with workforce outcomes of any high school characteristics variable and the pattern is largely flat across time. The urban/rural character of the high school from which a graduate came matters very little for long-run workforce outcomes in Southeast Minnesota, with the modest Year 5 postsecondary attendance difference being the most substantively interesting signal in the data.

EDR shows very weak associations with workforce outcomes across all three time points (V=0.017 at Year 1, V=0.049 at Year 5, V=0.055 at Year 10), with the unusual pattern of strengthening slightly over time rather than weakening — the opposite of most variables examined so far.

avg.unemp.rate

The average unemployment rate of the high school’s local area shows a statistically significant association with workforce outcomes at all three time points, with the strongest effect at Year 1 (F=107.7) and weakening at Years 5 (F=21.7) and 10 (F=59.0).

At Year 1, Tukey HSD comparisons show that graduates in meaningful regional employment come from areas with notably lower average unemployment than all other outcome groups. The largest gaps are between meaningful regional employment and no-MN-record (difference=0.190) and meaningful regional employment and non-meaningful employment (difference=0.146). Meaningful MN employment graduates come from the lowest unemployment areas of all — sitting 0.168 below meaningful regional employment — suggesting early out-of-region employment is associated with the most economically distressed home communities, possibly reflecting limited local opportunity driving earlier geographic mobility. Attending ps and non-meaningful employment are not significantly different from each other (adj. p=0.125).

By Year 5, the pattern partially reverses. Meaningful regional employment graduates no longer come from the lowest unemployment areas — meaningful MN employment graduates now come from significantly higher unemployment areas (difference=0.112 above regional). Non-meaningful employment is modestly above meaningful regional employment (difference=0.046), while attending ps and no-MN-record are not significantly different from meaningful regional employment. This suggests that by Year 5 higher local unemployment is associated with out-of-region employment rather than regional attachment.

By Year 10, meaningful regional employment graduates come from the lowest unemployment areas among the three active workforce categories. Meaningful MN employment (difference=0.174) and non-meaningful employment (difference=0.124) both come from significantly higher unemployment areas, and the no-MN-record group sits between these extremes (difference=0.066 above regional). The pattern is consistent with a long-run mechanism where stronger local labor markets anchor graduates regionally, while higher local unemployment pushes graduates toward out-of-region employment or labor force marginality.

Summary

The high school accomplishments group is the most analytically rich variable group examined so far, containing the strongest predictors of workforce outcomes in the entire analysis. The group divides clearly into two distinct clusters: academic achievement and orientation variables (took.ACT, ACTCompositeScore, ap.exam, MCA.R, MCA.M, MCA.S, pseo.participant) that are strongly associated with postsecondary pathways and out-of-region employment, and CTE engagement variables (cte.achievement, total.cte.courses.taken) that are strongly and uniquely associated with regional workforce attachment.

RANKING BY STRENGTH OF ASSOCIATION (Cramer’s V or F-statistic at Year 5 as primary sort)

  1. took.ACT (V=0.419 / 0.203 / 0.123) The strongest single categorical predictor in the entire analysis to this point. ACT participation functions as a near-perfect proxy for postsecondary orientation at Year 1, sorting graduates sharply into postsecondary attendance versus workforce entry. By Year 10, the no-MN record rate converges completely across ACT and non-ACT takers, confirming that geographic absence is not driven by academic disengagement but rather by out-of-state migration following credential completion among the highest achievers.

  2. MCA Math (F=4955.4 / 1487.9 / 317.0) The strongest continuous predictor and the highest F-statistics of any academic achievement variable at Years 1 and 5. MCA Math scores differentiate postsecondary attendees from workforce entrants at Year 1, and by Years 5 and 10 produce the clearest separation between the regional employment cluster (lower scores) and the MN employment plus no-MN-record cluster (higher scores). The convergence of meaningful MN employment and no-MN-record at similar high scores by Year 10 is the sharpest expression of this pattern across all academic measures.

  3. ap.exam (V=0.281 / 0.165 / 0.130) AP participation produces the largest postsecondary attendance gap of any variable at Year 1 (80.0% vs. 46.7%) and the largest no-MN-record gap at Year 10 (40.0% vs. 31.7%) — both reflections of AP participation selecting for the most academically oriented graduates who are most likely to complete four-year credentials and leave Minnesota. Its exclusion from non-college completer models is well-justified by the small group size in that subpopulation.

  4. MCA Reading (F=3405.6 / 395.2 / 812.6) MCA Reading is notable for its increasing F-statistic from Year 5 to Year 10 — the only academic achievement variable to show this pattern. The Year 10 finding is analytically important: meaningful MN employment and the no-MN-record group converge at virtually identical scores, both substantially above regional employment and non-meaningful employment. This two-cluster structure at Year 10 is the clearest evidence in the entire analysis that geographic mobility — whether captured in Minnesota records or not — is driven by high academic achievement.

  5. MCA Science (F=2162.5 / 440.1 / 181.4) MCA Science closely mirrors MCA Reading in structure throughout but with a more steeply declining F-statistic, consistent with voluntary assessment self-selection introducing composition effects. The Year 10 two-cluster structure — regional and non-meaningful at lower scores, MN employment and no-MN-record at higher scores — is identical to MCA Reading, reinforcing confidence in the finding. MCA Science is excluded from multivariate models due to the self-selection concern but serves as useful corroborating evidence in bivariate stages.

  6. pseo.participant (V=0.257 / 0.097 / 0.071) PSEO participation shows a strong Year 1 association driven by the postsecondary orientation of PSEO participants, but weakens more sharply than other variables over time. Its cohort restriction (2011+ only) and modest long-run effect size relative to other variables confirm the framework decision to treat it as supplementary rather than a primary analytical variable.

  7. cte.achievement (V=0.096 / 0.099 / 0.089) CTE achievement shows the flattest association trajectory of any variable in the group — its Cramer’s V values are virtually identical across all three time points. This stability is analytically distinctive: while academic orientation variables peak early and fade, CTE engagement maintains consistent associative strength across the full observation window. The dose-response gradient in meaningful regional employment (concentrators 34.9%, participants 30.5%, No CTE 22.2% at Year 10) is monotonic and durable, and the No CTE group’s elevated no-MN-record rate (39.3% at Year 10) confirms that absence of CTE engagement is the defining characteristic of the disconnected pathway.

  8. total.cte.courses.taken (F=1292.2 / 613.1 / 335.9) Total CTE courses taken produces the most consistent dose-response relationship of any variable in the group. Regional employment graduates take the most CTE courses at every time point, and the no-MN-record group takes the fewest — a gradient that strengthens rather than weakens over time. The Year 10 finding that the no-MN-record group takes significantly fewer CTE courses than even non-meaningful employment graduates is the strongest single piece of evidence that geographic absence from Minnesota’s labor market is associated with low CTE engagement rather than high academic achievement alone. This confirms the framework decision to use total.cte.courses.taken in non-college completer multivariate models.

CROSS-CUTTING THEMES

Two distinct and opposing pathways are visible across the accomplishment group. The college completer and geographic mobility pathway is defined by high academic achievement (ACT, MCA scores, AP participation, PSEO) and is associated with postsecondary attendance, credential completion, and sustained employment outside the region. The workforce-first pathway is defined by CTE engagement and is associated with early and sustained regional employment. These two pathways are not simply mirror images — they reflect genuinely different orientations toward education and work that manifest clearly by Year 1 and remain stable through Year 10.

The disconnected pathway, characterized by absence of both credential completion and sustained employment, is most strongly associated with low CTE engagement and low academic achievement simultaneously — the group that falls between both pathways rather than choosing one. The no-MN-record group at Year 10 shows a bimodal character: some portion reflects successful out-of-state migration among high academic achievers, while another portion reflects the geographic absence disconnection signature among low CTE engagers. Disentangling these two subpopulations within the no-MN-record category will be a central interpretive challenge in the narrative synthesis.


pseo.participant

PSEO participation shows a strong association at Year 1 (V=0.257) that weakens substantially by Years 5 and 10 (V=0.097 and V=0.071). Note that this variable is restricted to 2011+ cohorts and the NA group has been excluded, so the analysis covers approximately 91,000 graduates at Year 1 rather than the full population.

At Year 1, the dominant pattern is a striking postsecondary attendance gap — PSEO participants attend postsecondary at 72.6% compared to 45.6% for non-participants. This is expected given that PSEO participation is itself a postsecondary experience during high school and selects for academically oriented students. Non-participants show substantially higher rates of meaningful regional employment (16.6% vs. 9.7%), non-meaningful employment (19.7% vs. 10.0%), and no-MN-record (12.4% vs. 4.3%), all consistent with earlier workforce entry among the non-PSEO population.

By Year 5, the postsecondary attendance gap narrows but remains visible (22.2% vs. 16.3%). PSEO participants now show higher meaningful MN employment (19.4% vs. 15.6%) — consistent with college completion driving out-of-region career pathways — while non-participants hold a modest advantage in meaningful regional employment (28.2% vs. 24.9%). Non-participants also show higher non-meaningful employment (18.5% vs. 14.5%).

By Year 10, differences are modest (V=0.071). PSEO participants show higher meaningful MN employment (25.9% vs. 19.7%) and lower meaningful regional employment (25.6% vs. 29.7%), while no-MN-record rates are essentially equivalent (33.8% vs. 34.5%). Non-meaningful employment is slightly higher for non-participants (16.2% vs. 14.7%).

The overall pattern is consistent with PSEO participation serving as a strong proxy for postsecondary orientation and four-year credential pursuit. PSEO participants are more likely to complete credentials and find sustained employment outside the region, while non-participants enter the regional workforce earlier but at similar long-run regional employment rates. This confirms the framework decision to treat PSEO as a supplementary variable given its cohort restriction, noting its modest effect size relative to other accomplishment variables.

took.ACT

ACT participation shows one of the strongest associations of any variable examined so far, with a very high Cramer’s V at Year 1 (V=0.419) that weakens substantially but remains meaningful at Years 5 and 10 (V=0.203 and V=0.123).

Non-takers show dramatically higher rates of meaningful regional employment (20.3% vs. 11.2%), non-meaningful employment (27.7% vs. 11.9%), and no-MN-record (21.2% vs. 5.2%). ACT participation is functioning here as a near-perfect proxy for postsecondary orientation, reflecting self-selection rather than a direct causal mechanism.

By Year 5, the postsecondary gap narrows as ACT takers complete credentials and enter the labor market, but remains visible (23.2% vs. 8.8%). Non-takers now show higher meaningful regional employment (30.9% vs. 25.4%) and higher non-meaningful employment (21.7% vs. 15.7%) and no-MN-record rates (25.7% vs. 17.8%). ACT takers show meaningfully higher meaningful MN employment (17.9% vs. 13.0%), consistent with college-completion-driven out-of-region career pathways.

By Year 10, the no-MN-record rate converges completely (33.5% vs. 33.4%). Non-takers retain a modest advantage in meaningful regional employment (32.7% vs. 27.7%), while ACT takers show substantially higher meaningful MN employment (24.9% vs. 15.4%) and lower non-meaningful employment (14.1% vs. 18.4%).

The long-run picture is clear: ACT participation is strongly associated with college-completion pathways and sustained out-of-region employment, while non-ACT graduates who connect to the workforce are more likely to do so regionally. The complete convergence of no-MN-record rates by Year 10 is notable — the geographic absence signature is not systematically associated with ACT participation in the long run, suggesting it reflects out-of-state migration among college completers rather than disconnection among non-takers.

ACTCompositeScore

ACT composite score shows strong and consistent associations with workforce outcomes at all three time points (F=2177.4 at Year 1, F=639.8 at Year 5, F=455.8 at Year 10), with effect sizes remaining meaningful across the full observation window — unlike many variables that weaken sharply over time.

Meaningful regional and MN employment graduates score nearly identically (difference=-0.064, not significant), while non-meaningful employment graduates score 0.85 points below regional and no-MN-record graduates score 1.49 points below regional. This suggests that at Year 1, ACT score differentiates postsecondary attendees from the rest but does not meaningfully distinguish between the workforce outcome categories.

By Year 5, a more differentiated picture emerges. Meaningful regional employment graduates now have the lowest mean ACT score of any active workforce group. Meaningful MN employment (difference=1.84) and attending ps (difference=2.43) graduates score substantially higher, while the no-MN-record group also scores notably higher (difference=2.28) — suggesting higher-scoring graduates who have not yet found sustained MN employment may still be completing credentials or pursuing opportunities outside the state. Non-meaningful employment graduates score only modestly above regional (difference=0.71).

By Year 10, the pattern consolidates. Meaningful regional employment graduates have the lowest mean ACT scores, with meaningful MN employment (difference=1.64), non-meaningful employment (difference=0.81), and no-MN-record (difference=2.37) all scoring above them. The no-MN-record group now has the highest mean ACT scores of any group — substantially above even meaningful MN employment (difference=0.73) — suggesting that the highest-scoring graduates are the most likely to have left Minnesota’s formal employment records entirely, consistent with out-of-state migration following credential completion.

The long-run ACT pattern tells a consistent story: higher academic achievement is associated with college completion and out-of-region careers, while regional workforce attachment is most characteristic of graduates with lower ACT scores. This does not indicate a deficiency in regional employment — it reflects the sorting function of postsecondary education and the geography of college-driven career pathways.

MCA Reading score shows strong and sustained associations with workforce outcomes at all three time points (F=3405.6 at Year 1, F=395.2 at Year 5, F=812.6 at Year 10), with effect sizes remaining meaningful throughout — and notably increasing from Year 5 to Year 10.

MCA.S

MCA Science score shows strong associations at all three time points (F=2162.5 at Year 1, F=440.1 at Year 5, F=181.4 at Year 10), though the F-statistic declines more steeply over time than MCA Reading — consistent with MCA Science being a voluntary and self-selected assessment that introduces sample composition effects.

At Year 1, postsecondary attendees score 0.490 points above meaningful regional employment graduates — the largest gap of any academic achievement variable at this time point. Meaningful regional and MN employment graduates score nearly identically (difference=-0.016, not significant), while non-meaningful employment graduates score 0.072 below regional and no-MN-record graduates score 0.047 below regional (though the latter is only marginally significant). The Year 1 pattern mirrors MCA Reading closely.

By Year 5, meaningful regional employment graduates have the lowest mean MCA Science scores among active workforce participants. Meaningful MN employment (difference=0.221) and attending ps (difference=0.362) score substantially higher. The no-MN-record group scores above regional (difference=0.181) but below meaningful MN employment (difference=-0.039). Non-meaningful employment scores modestly below regional (difference=-0.036).

By Year 10, the pattern simplifies. Meaningful regional employment and non-meaningful employment graduates score essentially identically (difference=-0.013, p=0.833). Meaningful MN employment and the no-MN-record group both score approximately 0.23 points above regional, and are not significantly different from each other (difference=-0.013, p=0.767).

The Year 10 structure of MCA Science closely mirrors MCA Reading — regional employment and non-meaningful employment converge at lower scores, while meaningful MN employment and no-MN-record converge at higher scores. This parallel structure reinforces confidence in the finding and confirms that academic achievement in high school is consistently associated with geographic mobility rather than regional workforce attachment in the long run, regardless of which assessment is used as the measure.

MCA.M

MCA Math shows the strongest F-statistics of any academic achievement variable at Years 1 and 5 (F=4955.4 and F=1487.9), and remains strong at Year 10 (F=317.0). The overall pattern closely parallels MCA Reading and Science, with some notable differences in magnitude.

At Year 1, postsecondary attendees score 0.662 points above meaningful regional employment graduates — substantially larger than the comparable gaps for MCA Reading (0.342) and Science (0.490). Meaningful regional and MN employment graduates score essentially identically (difference=0.018, not significant). Non-meaningful employment graduates score 0.141 below regional and no-MN-record graduates score 0.216 below regional — both larger gaps than seen for MCA Reading, suggesting MCA Math is a somewhat stronger differentiator of early disconnection.

By Year 5, MCA Math produces the largest score gaps of any academic achievement variable. Meaningful MN employment (difference=0.534) and attending ps (difference=0.594) both score substantially above meaningful regional employment. The no-MN-record group scores modestly above regional (difference=0.135) but well below both MN employment and attending ps. Non-meaningful employment scores below regional (difference=-0.079).

By Year 10, the familiar two-cluster structure emerges. Meaningful regional employment and non-meaningful employment score essentially identically (difference=-0.010, p=0.917). Meaningful MN employment scores 0.333 above regional, and the no-MN-record group scores 0.300 above regional — these two are not significantly different from each other (difference=-0.033, p=0.096). The Year 10 gap magnitudes are larger for MCA Math than for MCA Reading (0.333/0.300 vs. 0.400/0.407) but the structural pattern is identical.

MCA Math is the strongest single academic achievement predictor in this analysis and shows the clearest separation between the college-completion and geographic mobility pathway on one hand, and the regional workforce and non-meaningful employment pathway on the other. The consistent convergence of meaningful regional employment and non-meaningful employment at the lower end of the score distribution across all three time points is a durable finding that will carry into the multivariate stages.

cte.achievement

CTE achievement shows a remarkably stable association with workforce outcomes across all three time points (V=0.096 at Year 1, V=0.099 at Year 5, V=0.089 at Year 10) — the flattest trajectory of any accomplishment variable examined. Unlike the academic achievement variables which peak at Year 1 and decline, CTE engagement maintains essentially the same associative strength across the entire observation window.

At Year 1, CTE concentrators and completors show the highest rate of meaningful regional employment (18.3%) — more than double the rate for No CTE graduates (8.7%) and well above CTE participants (12.9%). The inverse is true for postsecondary attendance: No CTE graduates attend at 59.9%, CTE participants at 57.2%, and concentrators at just 48.8%. The no-MN-record rate is notably lower for CTE participants (8.5%) than for both concentrators (10.1%) and No CTE graduates (12.1%), a pattern worth watching over time.

By Year 5, the CTE gradient in meaningful regional employment sharpens further. Concentrators reach 33.1%, participants 27.6%, and No CTE graduates just 19.6% — a 13.5 percentage point spread across the three categories. No CTE graduates show the highest no-MN-record rate (24.5%) compared to concentrators (18.2%) and participants (18.7%). Meaningful MN employment is essentially flat across all three CTE categories (15.7%, 16.3%, 17.1%), confirming that CTE engagement is specifically associated with regional workforce attachment rather than out-of-region employment.

By Year 10, the regional employment gradient remains strong and consistent: concentrators at 34.9%, participants at 30.5%, and No CTE graduates at 22.2%. The no-MN-record gap widens further — No CTE graduates reach 39.3% compared to 29.6% for concentrators and 32.5% for participants. Meaningful MN employment shows a modest upward gradient from concentrators to No CTE graduates (19.8%, 20.9%, 23.1%), consistent with No CTE graduates being more likely to pursue college-completion pathways that lead out of the region.

CTE achievement is the defining variable for the workforce-first pathway in Southeast Minnesota. Its association with regional employment is monotonic, dose-responsive, and stable across all three time points — a pattern unmatched by any other variable in this group. The absence of a CTE engagement advantage in meaningful MN employment confirms that CTE’s effect is specifically regional in character. The No CTE group’s elevated no-MN-record rate at Year 10 (39.3%) adds further weight to the interpretation that absence of CTE engagement is the defining characteristic of the disconnected pathway.

total.cte.courses.taken

Total CTE courses taken shows strong and sustained associations with workforce outcomes at all three time points (F=1292.2 at Year 1, F=613.1 at Year 5, F=335.9 at Year 10), with meaningful regional employment graduates consistently taking the most CTE courses of any outcome group across the entire observation window.

At Year 1, meaningful regional employment graduates have the highest mean CTE course count of any group. All other groups score below them: meaningful MN employment (difference=-0.892), non-meaningful employment (difference=-0.927), no-MN-record (difference=-1.248), and postsecondary attendees (difference=-1.729). Notably, meaningful MN employment and non-meaningful employment are not significantly different from each other (difference=-0.035, p=0.935), while the no-MN-record group takes significantly fewer courses than either employment group.

By Year 5, the ordering is maintained and the gaps are substantively meaningful. Meaningful regional employment remains at the top. Meaningful MN employment (difference=-0.836) and attending ps (difference=-1.215) score below regional, while non-meaningful employment (difference=-0.405) falls between regional and MN employment. The no-MN-record group scores substantially below regional (difference=-1.081) and also below meaningful MN employment (difference=-0.245).

By Year 10, the pattern is fully consolidated. Meaningful regional employment graduates have taken approximately 0.81 more CTE courses than meaningful MN employment graduates, 0.55 more than non-meaningful employment graduates, and 0.96 more than the no-MN-record group. The no-MN-record group now takes significantly fewer courses than even non-meaningful employment graduates (difference=-0.404).

Total CTE courses taken produces the clearest and most consistent dose-response relationship of any variable in the accomplishment group. Higher CTE course-taking is monotonically associated with regional workforce attachment at all three time points, and the absence of CTE engagement is most strongly associated with geographic absence from Minnesota’s formal labor market. This confirms the framework decision to use total.cte.courses.taken rather than cte.achievement in the non-college completer multivariate models, where its continuous dose-response structure provides superior sensitivity.

Summary

The postsecondary variable group is the most structurally complex of the four groups, containing variables that are both outcomes and predictors, overlapping by construction, and operating through fundamentally different mechanisms depending on credential type and geographic location. The group’s defining contribution to the analysis is clarifying how the college completer pathway operates — and specifically how graduation location, credential level, and institution type jointly determine whether postsecondary completion translates into regional workforce attachment or geographic departure.

RANKING BY STRENGTH OF ASSOCIATION (Cramer’s V at Year 5 as primary sort)

  1. ps.grad.location (V=0.164 / 0.208 / 0.171) The strongest and most analytically important postsecondary variable, and the only one in the entire analysis whose association strengthens from Year 1 to Year 5. Graduation location is the critical hinge of the college completer pathway: regional graduation is strongly associated with meaningful regional employment at all three time points (63.0%, 52.7%, 43.2%), MN graduation is strongly associated with meaningful MN employment (26.3%, 43.4%, 37.9%), and outside MN graduation is associated with geographic absence from Minnesota’s formal labor market — reaching a 54.2% no-MN-record rate by Year 10. This variable provides the clearest evidence in the entire analysis that the no-MN-record group is heterogeneous, with outside MN graduates representing successful out-of-state career establishment rather than disconnection.

  2. attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad (V=0.254 / 0.118 / 0.074) The timing of postsecondary enrollment matters most at Year 1, where immediate enrollees show substantially higher rates of concurrent workforce participation than delayed or non-enrollees. By Year 10 the distinction between immediate and delayed enrollment largely disappears, with postsecondary attendance of any timing providing a modest advantage over non-attendance primarily through access to out-of-region career pathways. This variable will be excluded from later analytical stages given its cohort restriction and overlap with attended.ps.

  3. attended.ps (V=0.285 / 0.164 / 0.095) Postsecondary attendance shows a moderate and declining association driven primarily by its role as an academic orientation indicator. Its most durable signal is in meaningful MN employment — postsecondary attenders show consistently higher out-of-region employment through Year 10 — confirming that attending postsecondary, even without completing a credential, provides some access to out-of-region career pathways. Its primary analytical role is as a predictor in the non-college completer multivariate models.

  4. ps.grad.InstitutionSector (V=0.184 / 0.150 / 0.117) Institution sector reveals that the college completer pathway operates very differently by institution type. Public 2-year graduates show the strongest regional employment at all three time points (55.1%, 41.4%, 44.0%), while private not-for-profit 4-year graduates show the highest no-MN-record rates (26.0%, 22.4%, 41.8%) and meaningful MN employment. The sector findings directly anticipate the ps.pathway combined variable structure for the multivariate stages and confirm the importance of disaggregating postsecondary pathways by both credential type and institution selectivity.

  5. ps.grad (V=0.176 / 0.119 / 0.120) Postsecondary graduation shows a stable moderate association across all three time points — unusual in its non-declining trajectory. The key finding is that credential completion is specifically associated with meaningful MN employment rather than regional employment, which converges between graduates and non-graduates by Year 10. Non-graduates show persistently elevated non-meaningful employment — the local underutilization disconnection signature — suggesting that postsecondary attendance without completion carries a durable labor market penalty.

  6. highest.cred.level (V=0.171 / 0.138 / 0.112) Credential level reveals a non-linear relationship with regional employment that is one of the most important findings in the postsecondary group. Sub-baccalaureate credential completion — the less than bachelor’s category — is most strongly associated with regional employment at all three time points (52.5%, 47.6%, 40.6%), while bachelor’s or higher completion is most strongly associated with meaningful MN employment and the highest no-MN-record rates. This non-linearity confirms that the college completer pathway bifurcates sharply by credential level and reinforces the sub-baccalaureate versus baccalaureate distinction built into the ps.pathway variable structure.

CROSS-CUTTING THEMES

The postsecondary group tells a coherent story that organizes around three mechanisms. First, sub-baccalaureate credential completion in the region is the most direct postsecondary route to sustained regional employment — more reliable than four-year completion and more durable than workforce entry without a credential. Second, four-year credential completion outside the region is the primary driver of geographic departure from Minnesota, with outside-MN graduation producing a 54.2% no-MN-record rate at Year 10 that should be interpreted as out-of-state career establishment rather than disconnection. Third, postsecondary attendance without completion produces the most persistently negative outcomes — elevated non-meaningful employment and moderate no-MN-record rates — representing a failed attempt at the college completer pathway that leaves graduates without either the credential advantages of completers or the early workforce advantages of those who never attempted postsecondary.

The confirmed redundancy across attended.ps, ps.grad, highest.cred.level, ps.grad.location, and ps.grad.InstitutionSector — visible in both the MCA and across the bivariate stage — reinforces the framework decision to consolidate these variables into the ps.pathway combined variable for multivariate modeling rather than entering them simultaneously. The bivariate findings here provide the substantive foundation for how ps.pathway categories should be defined and interpreted in Stages 7 and 8.

This variable has three categories: Did not attend ps (never attended or attended only to withdraw), No (attended postsecondary but not within the first year), and Yes (attended within the first year). The attending ps outcome category is excluded from these tables given that virtually all postsecondary attendees are captured within the independent variable itself. Immediate enrollees show substantially higher meaningful MN employment (23.1% vs. 17.6% for delayed and 13.4% for non-attenders) and lower no-MN-record rates (21.3% vs. 22.9% for delayed and 33.7% for non-attenders). Non-attenders show the highest no-MN-record rate (38.9%) followed by delayed enrollees (34.0%) and immediate enrollees (31.5%).

The overall pattern confirms that timing of postsecondary enrollment matters most at Year 1, where immediate enrollment is associated with early workforce entry alongside postsecondary attendance. By Year 10, postsecondary attendance of any timing provides a modest advantage in sustained employment outcomes relative to non-attendance, but the distinction between immediate and delayed enrollment largely disappears.

highest.cred.level

Highest credential level shows a moderate and declining association with workforce outcomes (V=0.171 at Year 1, V=0.138 at Year 5, V=0.112 at Year 10). The four categories — no college, some college, less than bachelor’s, and bachelor’s or higher — reveal a complex and non-linear pattern in which sub-baccalaureate credential completion is most strongly associated with regional employment, while bachelor’s or higher completion is most strongly associated with out-of-region employment.

At Year 1, among those not currently attending postsecondary, less than bachelor’s credential holders show the highest meaningful regional employment (52.5%), followed by bachelor’s or higher graduates (45.4%), some college (36.9%), and no college (22.2%). The less than bachelor’s group also shows the lowest no-MN-record rate (8.5%), while the no-college group shows the highest (29.5%). Meaningful MN employment increases monotonically with credential level — from 7.6% for no college to 16.3% for bachelor’s or higher.

By Year 5, the credential gradient in meaningful regional employment sharpens. Less than bachelor’s holders reach 47.6% — the highest of any credential category — while bachelor’s or higher graduates show only 29.0%, actually below the some college group (35.9%). Bachelor’s or higher graduates show by far the highest meaningful MN employment (29.1% vs. 20.2% for less than bachelor’s and 16.6% for some college), and a relatively high no-MN-record rate (24.5%) suggesting substantial out-of-state migration.

By Year 10, the non-linear credential pattern is fully consolidated. Less than bachelor’s holders show the highest meaningful regional employment (40.6%), followed by some college (32.8%), no college (28.3%), and bachelor’s or higher (24.0%). Bachelor’s or higher graduates show the highest meaningful MN employment (27.6%) and the highest no-MN-record rate (36.3%), consistent with four-year credential completion driving both out-of-region career success and out-of-state migration. Non-meaningful employment declines monotonically with credential level (17.8%, 19.5%, 15.5%, 12.1%), with bachelor’s or higher showing the lowest rate.

The non-linear relationship between credential level and regional employment is the central finding for this variable. Sub-baccalaureate credential completion — the less than bachelor’s category, which includes associate degrees and technical certificates — is most strongly associated with sustained regional employment at every time point. This directly supports the institution sector findings and confirms that the college completer pathway operates through very different mechanisms depending on whether the credential is sub-baccalaureate or baccalaureate in nature.

ps.grad.location

Graduation location is the strongest postsecondary variable examined and shows an unusual pattern of strengthening association over time (V=0.164 at Year 1, V=0.208 at Year 5, V=0.171 at Year 10). It is also the variable most directly relevant to the college completer pathway framework, where graduation location serves as the critical hinge determining whether credential completion translates into regional or out-of-region workforce attachment.

At Year 1, regional graduates show by far the highest meaningful regional employment (63.0%) — more than double the rate for MN graduates (32.6%) and outside MN graduates (30.9%). Regional graduates also show the lowest no-MN-record rate (5.3%) and lowest non-meaningful employment (18.6%). MN graduates show notably high meaningful MN employment (26.3%), consistent with out-of-region but in-state credential completion translating into in-state employment. Outside MN graduates show an elevated no-MN-record rate (27.3%) at Year 1, likely reflecting concurrent enrollment or recent graduation rather than sustained out-of-state employment.

By Year 5, the graduation location signal is at its strongest. Regional graduates maintain a dominant meaningful regional employment advantage (52.7% vs. 23.9% for MN graduates and 21.4% for outside MN graduates). MN graduates show a striking meaningful MN employment rate (43.4%) — by far the highest of any location category — confirming that in-state out-of-region graduation is strongly associated with sustained in-state out-of-region employment. Outside MN graduates show the highest no-MN-record rate of any credential group at Year 5 (45.7%), consistent with sustained out-of-state employment following out-of-state credential completion.

By Year 10, the patterns consolidate further. Regional graduates maintain the highest meaningful regional employment (43.2%) — nearly twice the rate for MN graduates (20.5%) and outside MN graduates (17.8%). MN graduates continue to show the highest meaningful MN employment (37.9%), and outside MN graduates show the highest no-MN-record rate of any category (54.2%) — more than half of outside-MN credential completers have no Minnesota employment record a full decade after high school graduation. Did-not-graduate graduates show the highest non-meaningful employment (18.7%) and a substantial no-MN-record rate (34.0%).

Graduation location is the clearest expression of the college completer pathway’s geographic sorting mechanism in the entire analysis. Regional graduation produces regional employment; MN graduation produces MN employment; outside MN graduation produces geographic absence from Minnesota’s formal labor market. The outside MN no-MN-record rate of 54.2% at Year 10 is not a disconnection indicator but rather the most direct evidence that a substantial portion of Southeast Minnesota’s high school graduates are successfully establishing careers outside the state following out-of-state credential completion. This distinction will be essential to the narrative synthesis.

Summary

OVERVIEW

The bivariate analysis of Southeast Minnesota high school graduates across four variable groups — demographics, high school characteristics, high school accomplishments, and postsecondary — reveals a consistent and coherent structure that maps onto the three-pathway framework with considerable clarity. The findings are organized around two fundamental sorting mechanisms: postsecondary orientation, which drives geographic departure from the region, and CTE engagement, which drives regional workforce attachment. A third group — those who neither complete credentials nor achieve sustained employment — is defined primarily by the absence of both mechanisms.

WORKFORCE OUTCOME DISTRIBUTION

The baseline distribution establishes the interpretive context for all that follows. At Year 1, the population is dominated by postsecondary attendance (54.4%), reflecting the high educational orientation of Southeast Minnesota graduates. By Year 5, postsecondary attendance contracts sharply to 18.5% as graduates complete or exit credentials, and meaningful regional employment rises to 27.2%. By Year 10, postsecondary attendance is essentially zero, meaningful regional employment reaches 29.7%, and the most notable feature is the large and growing no-MN-record group (33.4%). This group is the central interpretive challenge of the analysis — it is heterogeneous, comprising both successful out-of-state migrants and genuinely disconnected individuals, and its composition varies systematically across independent variables in ways that distinguish these two populations.

VARIABLE GROUP SUMMARIES

Demographics produced moderate associations, strongest at Year 1 where postsecondary sorting dominates the distribution. Special education status and economic disadvantage are the strongest demographic predictors, both associated with reduced postsecondary participation and elevated non-meaningful employment. Language minority variables — english.learner, non.english.home, and LimitedEnglishProficiencyIndicator — show a consistent and analytically interesting anomaly: elevated meaningful MN employment at Year 1, likely reflecting concentration in specific industries or urban labor markets. The most important demographic finding at Year 10 is the elevated no-MN-record rate for racial and ethnic minority graduates, particularly Asian/PI (45.1%), American Indian (41.1%), and Black (39.7%) graduates — a pattern more consistent with out-of-state migration following postsecondary completion than with genuine disconnection.

High school characteristics is the weakest variable group in the analysis, with the partial exception of average local unemployment rate. Community demographic description and EDR show negligible practical associations with workforce outcomes, and average wages as a percent of state average adds no meaningful explanatory power. The unemployment rate finding is substantively meaningful: stronger local labor markets anchor graduates regionally, while higher local unemployment is associated with out-of-region employment or labor force marginality — a long-run mechanism that becomes clearer at Years 5 and 10. The near-absence of place-based effects in this group suggests that individual-level characteristics and postsecondary pathways carry the explanatory weight in Southeast Minnesota.

High school accomplishments is the richest variable group and contains the strongest predictors in the entire analysis. The group divides into two distinct clusters with opposing relationships to workforce outcomes. The academic orientation cluster — took.ACT, ACT composite score, ap.exam, MCA Reading, MCA Math, MCA Science, and PSEO participation — is strongly and consistently associated with postsecondary attendance, credential completion, and sustained out-of-region employment. Higher academic achievement at the extreme is associated with geographic departure from Minnesota entirely — the no-MN-record group at Year 10 has among the highest ACT and MCA scores of any outcome category. The CTE engagement cluster — cte.achievement and total.cte.courses.taken — shows the opposite pattern: a stable, monotonic, dose-responsive association with meaningful regional employment that persists across all three time points with essentially no decay. CTE concentrators show 34.9% meaningful regional employment at Year 10; No CTE graduates show 22.2%. This 12.7 percentage point gap, stable from Year 1 through Year 10, is the single most consistent finding in the entire bivariate analysis.

The postsecondary group clarifies the mechanisms through which postsecondary pathways translate into workforce outcomes. The central finding is the non-linear relationship between credential level and regional employment — sub-baccalaureate completion is most strongly associated with regional workforce attachment, while baccalaureate and higher completion is most strongly associated with out-of-region employment and geographic departure. Graduation location is the strongest postsecondary variable and the only one that strengthens over time: regional graduation produces regional employment, MN graduation produces MN employment, and outside MN graduation produces a 54.2% no-MN-record rate at Year 10 — the clearest single data point establishing that geographic absence from Minnesota’s labor market reflects out-of-state career establishment among college completers rather than disconnection.

THE THREE PATHWAYS

The bivariate evidence supports a clear three-pathway structure.

The college completer pathway is defined by academic orientation in high school — ACT participation, high MCA scores, AP and PSEO participation — followed by postsecondary enrollment and credential completion. The critical hinge within this pathway is graduation location. Regional sub-baccalaureate completion produces sustained regional employment and is the postsecondary route most closely aligned with the workforce-first pathway. Out-of-region completion — particularly four-year completion outside Minnesota — produces sustained out-of-state employment and accounts for a substantial portion of the no-MN-record population at Year 10.

The workforce-first pathway is defined by CTE engagement in high school and direct entry into the regional labor market. It is the most stable pathway in the analysis — its defining variable (total CTE courses taken) shows the most consistent dose-response relationship across all three time points of any predictor examined. CTE concentrators and completors achieve meaningful regional employment at rates that exceed all other high school accomplishment profiles by Year 5 and Year 10. This pathway is not defined by academic disengagement — it coexists with moderate academic achievement — but by a deliberate orientation toward vocational preparation and regional labor market entry.

The disconnected pathway is defined by the simultaneous absence of both mechanisms — neither substantial CTE engagement nor postsecondary credential completion. It manifests as two distinct signatures. The local underutilization signature — present in the labor market but below the sustained employment threshold — is most strongly associated with special education status, economic disadvantage, and the No CTE group, and persists through Year 10 as non-meaningful employment. The geographic absence signature — no MN employment record — is the more complex of the two: it is driven partly by successful out-of-state migration among high academic achievers and partly by genuine disconnection among low-CTE, low-achievement graduates. The No CTE group shows a 39.3% no-MN-record rate at Year 10 — the highest of any CTE category and substantially above what can be explained by out-of-state migration alone — confirming that absence of CTE engagement is the defining characteristic of the genuine disconnection signature.

IMPLICATIONS FOR MULTIVARIATE STAGES

The bivariate analysis establishes clear expectations for the multivariate stages. CTE course-taking should emerge as the dominant predictor of sustained regional employment in the non-college completer models, replicating the Southwest Minnesota benchmark (OR≈1.055 per course at Years 1, 5, and 10). Graduation location should emerge as the dominant structural variable in the full population model, with regional sub-baccalaureate graduation showing the strongest regional employment odds. Academic achievement variables — particularly MCA Reading and took.ACT — should show negative associations with regional employment net of postsecondary pathway, reflecting the sorting function of academic orientation toward out-of-region careers. The unemployment rate should show a modest but consistent negative association with regional employment across both model sets. Demographic variables are expected to show attenuated effects in multivariate context given their substantial overlap with high school accomplishment and postsecondary pathway variables, with economic disadvantage and special education status the most likely to retain independent associations.


VARIABLE REVIEW PRIOR TO MULTIVARIATE ANALYSIS

VARIABLES TO EXCLUDE — ALREADY ESTABLISHED BY FRAMEWORK

The following exclusions are already built into the analytical framework and confirmed by the bivariate findings:

MCA.S: Voluntary and self-selected assessment excluding roughly 30% of the population. Its bivariate pattern was essentially identical to MCA.R and MCA.M throughout, adding no independent information.

non.english.home: Redundant with english.learner in both the MCA and bivariate stages. Weaker effect sizes at every time point. Excluded from multivariate models but retained for descriptive purposes.

avg.wages.pct.state: Negligible practical effect sizes throughout despite statistical significance. Confirmed as analytically meaningless.

pseo.participant: Cohort restriction (2011+) limits coverage. Confirmed as a modest predictor with effect sizes well below other accomplishment variables.

ap.exam: Excluded from non-college completer multivariate models due to small group size in that subpopulation. Retained in full population models.

VARIABLES TO EXCLUDE — SUPPORTED BY BIVARIATE FINDINGS

LimitedEnglishProficiencyIndicator: The weakest demographic predictor throughout (V=0.079 / 0.032 / 0.031), adding no explanatory power beyond english.learner. Its MCA loading on a separate dimension suggested it captures a distinct but narrow subpopulation. Not worth the degrees of freedom in a multivariate model.

attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad: Overlaps structurally with attended.ps and becomes essentially indistinguishable from it by Year 5. Its Year 1 signal is largely an artifact of concurrent enrollment timing rather than a substantive independent predictor. Exclude from all multivariate stages.

Dem_Desc: The weakest variable in the high school characteristics group (V=0.041 / 0.046 / 0.030) and essentially flat across time. Its bivariate associations are negligible in practical terms and it is likely to be absorbed entirely by edr in a multivariate context given their geographic overlap. Not worth including.
