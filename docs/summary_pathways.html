The pathways

THE WORKFORCE TRAJECTORIES OF SOUTHEAST MINNESOTA HIGH SCHOOL GRADUATES: A SYNTHESIS OF THE FULL ANALYSIS

Southeast Minnesota Workforce Pathway Study EDR 9 (South Central) and EDR 10 (Southeast) 2008–2023 High School Graduate Cohorts

THREE PATHWAYS FROM ONE STARTING POINT

Every graduate in this study started in the same place — a Southeast Minnesota high school classroom. Within a decade of leaving it, their economic trajectories had diverged dramatically. Some completed college credentials and established careers, in the region or elsewhere. Some entered the regional workforce directly and built sustained employment without a credential. And some — nearly half of those who did not complete a credential — spent most of their first five years after high school disconnected from sustained employment entirely.

This study followed 80,878 high school graduates across those diverging trajectories, using bivariate analysis, persistence frameworks, refined year-by-year outcome tracking, and multivariate logistic regression to identify what drove the divergence. The findings are consistent, coherent, and actionable. Three distinct pathways emerge from the data. Each is shaped by a recognizable set of forces — some rooted in high school experiences, some in demographic and structural circumstances, some in postsecondary choices. Understanding those forces, and the variables most strongly associated with each pathway, is the central contribution of this analysis.

PATHWAY 1: THE COLLEGE COMPLETER PATHWAY

WHO FOLLOWS THIS PATHWAY

Approximately 59% of Southeast Minnesota high school graduates complete a postsecondary credential. They are not a random cross- section of graduates. The college completer pathway is shaped by a specific constellation of high school characteristics that are visible years before graduation, and by structural demographic forces that determine access to completion even among those who attempt it.

WHAT PREDICTS ENTRY INTO THIS PATHWAY

Academic preparation is the primary predictor of postsecondary credential completion. In the multivariate models, ACT participation, AP exam participation, and higher MCA scores in math and reading all independently increase the odds of credential completion. These variables work in concert — graduates with multiple academic orientation markers are substantially more likely to complete. The dose-response relationship is clear: the more deeply a graduate was engaged with college-preparatory programming in high school, the more likely they were to complete a credential.

Gender is a significant predictor — females are more likely to complete credentials than males, net of all other characteristics. This is one of the most consistent findings in the full population analysis and reflects a national pattern. Among completers, females are more likely to hold bachelor’s degrees or higher; males are more likely to hold sub-baccalaureate credentials.

Economic disadvantage is a large and independent barrier to completion. Low-income graduates have approximately 66% lower odds of credential completion than non-low-income graduates in the fully adjusted model. This is among the largest economic status effects in the entire analysis and confirms that economic disadvantage is a powerful and independent obstacle to postsecondary completion in Southeast Minnesota — not simply a proxy for academic preparation differences.

Special education status is the strongest single demographic barrier to credential completion, with special education graduates showing approximately 77% lower odds of completion in the multivariate model. This effect operates independently of economic status and race.

Race and ethnicity shows wide variation. American Indian graduates have the lowest completion odds — approximately one-fifth the odds of White graduates in the adjusted model. Hispanic and Black graduates also show substantially lower completion odds. Asian/PI graduates are the only minority group showing higher completion odds than White graduates. These racial and ethnic gaps in completion are not fully explained by academic preparation or economic status — they reflect structural barriers that persist in the multivariate framework.

CTE engagement, notably, predicts sub-baccalaureate completion specifically. Graduates who concentrated in CTE programs are substantially more likely to complete at the two-year or technical certificate level — a finding that connects the college completer pathway directly to the workforce-first pathway through the mechanism of sub-baccalaureate regional completion.

WITHIN THE PATHWAY: WHERE YOU GRADUATE DETERMINES WHERE YOU WORK

The most important finding about college completers is not who completes — it is where. Among the 59% who complete credentials, graduation location is the critical hinge that determines whether completion translates into regional workforce attachment or geographic departure.

Regional completers — those who earn their credentials at institutions in Southeast Minnesota — show by far the highest rates of sustained regional employment. Sub-baccalaureate regional completion is the strongest single postsecondary predictor of regional employment in the entire multivariate analysis (OR=2.479). Bachelor’s or higher regional completion is close behind (OR=2.168). Regional credential completion, at any level, is the most direct postsecondary route to regional workforce attachment.

Out-of-region completion tells the opposite story. Graduates who complete credentials outside Minnesota — whether bachelor’s or sub-baccalaureate — show the lowest rates of sustained regional employment (ORs of 0.556 and 0.576 for bachelor’s pathways) and the highest rates of no-Minnesota employment record. By Year 10, 54.2% of outside-Minnesota completers have no Minnesota employment record — not because they are disconnected, but because they have established careers elsewhere. This is geographic departure, not labor market failure.

Who completes regionally and who leaves? Low-income and special education completers complete regionally at substantially higher rates than their non-disadvantaged peers — 52.6% and 54.0% respectively, compared to 37.9% and 39.9% for their counterparts. This reflects geographic access constraints: economic and institutional barriers concentrate disadvantaged graduates at nearby regional institutions. High academic achievers — high ACT scorers, AP exam participants — are more likely to complete outside Minnesota, particularly out of state. CTE concentrators are significantly more likely to complete regionally.

EDR matters here in a way it rarely does elsewhere in the analysis. EDR 10 graduates complete outside Minnesota at 32.7% compared to just 24.4% for EDR 9 graduates, while EDR 9 graduates complete sub-baccalaureate credentials within Minnesota at nearly double the EDR 10 rate. This geographic sorting is the mechanism behind the workforce outcome divergence between the two EDRs — EDR 9’s credential completion pattern keeps more graduates in Minnesota and more graduates in the region.

THE COLLEGE COMPLETER PATHWAY IN SUMMARY

Entry into the college completer pathway is predicted by academic preparation (ACT, AP, MCA scores), female gender, non-low-income status, non-special education status, and White or Asian/PI race and ethnicity. Within the pathway, the destination that matters most is not credential level but completion location — regional completion at any level is the strongest postsecondary predictor of sustained regional employment. Sub-baccalaureate regional completion is the single most direct route from high school to regional workforce attachment in Southeast Minnesota.

PATHWAY 2: THE WORKFORCE-FIRST PATHWAY

WHO FOLLOWS THIS PATHWAY

Among the 41% of graduates who do not complete a postsecondary credential, a meaningful subset achieves sustained regional employment without one. By Year 5, approximately 30% of non- completers are in sustained regional employment. By Year 10, that figure reaches approximately 32%. These are the workforce- first graduates — individuals who entered the regional labor market directly from high school, with or without a postsecondary attempt, and who established durable employment in Southeast Minnesota without completing a credential.

WHAT PREDICTS ENTRY INTO THIS PATHWAY

Career and technical education engagement is the primary and most durable predictor of workforce-first success. The finding is consistent, large, and confirmed across every analytical framework in the study. In the non-completer multivariate models, CTE Concentrators and Completors have 64% higher odds of sustained regional employment at Year 1 compared to No CTE graduates, growing to 74% higher at Year 5 and holding at 66% higher at Year 10. Every pairwise comparison in the Tukey analysis of total CTE courses taken is significant at every time point — this is a true dose-response relationship, not a threshold effect. More CTE is better, and the advantage does not fade across a decade.

What makes CTE’s role analytically distinctive is its specificity to regional attachment. CTE engagement predicts sustained regional employment. It does not predict sustained out-of-region employment. CTE prepares graduates for the specific contours of the Southeast Minnesota regional labor market — the trades, technical fields, manufacturing, and health occupations that constitute the regional economy’s demand — and this preparation produces a durable advantage that accumulated work experience does not erode.

Postsecondary attendance — even without completion — is the second most powerful predictor of workforce-first success at Year 1, and it remains significant throughout the decade. Non-completers who attempted postsecondary have more than double the odds of sustained regional employment at Year 1 (OR=2.023) compared to those who never attended. This advantage attenuates — to 45% at Year 5 and 28% at Year 10 — but it never disappears. Partial postsecondary experience provides human capital gains, employer network access, and labor market orientation that translate into better regional employment outcomes even without credential attainment. The policy implication is clear: supported postsecondary transitions, even for graduates unlikely to complete, produce measurable and lasting regional workforce benefits.

Male gender is a consistent predictor of workforce-first attachment. Males have 20–25% higher odds of sustained regional employment across all three time points in the non-completer models, reflecting the concentration of male non-completers in trades, construction, and manufacturing occupations that offer immediate full-time regional employment without credential requirements.

ACT participation shows a consistent but counterintuitive negative association with sustained regional employment among non-completers (OR≈0.851–0.910 across time points). ACT-takers are not less capable — they are more mobile. They are more likely to seek and achieve sustained employment outside the region rather than within it. This ACT mobility effect strengthens over the decade, reaching its largest magnitude at Year 10. Among non-completers who do achieve sustained employment, the ACT participation signal consistently points away from the region.

What does NOT predict this pathway is equally instructive. Place-based characteristics — which EDR, which community type, local wage levels — have no meaningful independent association with sustained regional employment among non-completers. Where a graduate attended high school within Southeast Minnesota does not determine whether they achieve regional workforce attachment. Individual preparation does. The one exception — average local unemployment rate — functions as a push factor rather than a barrier, associated with graduates seeking employment outside the region rather than with worse outcomes overall.

THE WORKFORCE-FIRST PATHWAY IN SUMMARY

Entry into this pathway is most powerfully predicted by CTE depth, followed by postsecondary attendance even without completion, and male gender. The pathway is specifically a regional attachment pathway — CTE prepares graduates for the regional economy, partial postsecondary experience provides labor market connectivity, and the combination of the two produces the most durable regional workforce attachment in the non-completer population. Importantly, the CTE advantage grows over the decade while the postsecondary attendance advantage attenuates — by Year 5, CTE is already the stronger predictor, and by Year 10 the gap has widened further. CTE depth is the most durable individual-level predictor of sustained regional employment in the entire analysis, across both the completer and non-completer populations.

PATHWAY 3: THE DISCONNECTED PATHWAY — AND ITS TWO DISTINCT POPULATIONS

WHO FOLLOWS THIS PATHWAY

Nearly half of all non-completers — 46.5% — are persistently disconnected from sustained employment in the first five years after exiting formal education, meaning they are disconnected in four or five of those years. This is the modal outcome for non-completers, not the exception. But the persistently disconnected population is not a single group. It divides into two qualitatively different subpopulations with different demographic profiles, different labor market relationships, and different implications for workforce policy. Understanding this distinction is the most practically important finding of the entire study.

THE TWO POPULATIONS WITHIN PATHWAY 3

Population 3A: The Locally Underutilized

The first disconnected population is locally present but not sustainably attached. These graduates appear in Minnesota wage records. They work — sometimes consistently, sometimes intermittently — in the region. But they do not accumulate into sustained employment. They cycle through part-time, seasonal, or low-wage employment without achieving the threshold that defines workforce success in this analysis. They are the graduates in the in-region marginal employment category — Non-sustained WF_in_only — and across both the persistence and refined year-by-year analyses, they are disproportionately:

Female non-completers, who are overrepresented in local marginal employment at every time point throughout the five-year window. The gender gap in in-region marginal employment grows over time: from a 1.5-point female overrepresentation at Year 1 to a 2.0-point gap by Year 5. Female non-completers are not absent from the regional labor market — they are present but unable to achieve sustained attachment.

Low-income non-completers, who show the most distinctive accumulating pattern in this category. At Year 1, low- income and non-low-income non-completers appear in in-region marginal employment at essentially identical rates (19.8% vs. 19.9%). By Year 5, low-income graduates are 2.5 percentage points more concentrated in this category, and by Year 5 the economic status Cramer’s V has nearly doubled from its Year 1 value. Low-income non-completers who remain in Minnesota are increasingly concentrated in local marginal employment over time — a compounding local underutilization that accumulates rather than resolves.

Special education graduates, who show persistent overrepresentation in in-region marginal employment throughout the observation window and whose no-record rates are simultaneously elevated. Special education non-completers show one in three with no Minnesota employment record at every time point, combined with higher-than-expected local marginal employment — a dual signature that reflects the breadth of labor market disadvantage this group faces.

White non-completers relative to minority peers, which is a counterintuitive finding: White graduates are more likely than most minority groups to appear in in-region marginal employment. This is because minority non- completers are more concentrated in the no-record category — they are not locally underutilized, they are geographically absent.

No CTE graduates, relative to CTE participants and concentrators, who are less likely to appear in local marginal employment not because they are doing better but because they are more likely to be in the no-record category. CTE engagement protects against both forms of disconnection, but its absence produces geographic absence more than local underutilization.

Population 3A is the reachable disconnected population for regional workforce development. These graduates have Minnesota wage records. They can be identified through UI wage data and DEED workforce systems. They are in the region. What they need is not outreach and connection — they are already connected to the local labor market in a marginal way. What they need is upskilling, career pathway navigation, and employer connection that can convert marginal employment into sustained attachment. CTE-linked apprenticeship, on-the-job training programs, industry-recognized credential attainment, and stackable certification pathways are the interventions most likely to move this population from local underutilization into sustained regional employment.

Population 3B: The Geographically Absent

The second disconnected population is largely invisible to traditional workforce development systems. These graduates have no Minnesota employment record — or records so thin as to be functionally absent — at multiple time points across the five-year and ten-year windows. They are not locally underutilized. They are not cycling through marginal employment. They simply do not appear in the measurable Minnesota labor market at rates that reflect their presence in the population. They are disproportionately:

English learner non-completers, who show the most extreme and growing geographic absence of any demographic group. More than four in ten English learner non-completers have no Minnesota employment record by Year 5 — a rate that grows over the observation window, not shrinks. In the multivariate models, English learner status starts as a non- significant predictor at Year 1 (its bivariate effect fully absorbed by racial and ethnic group membership) and emerges as a large, independent, and growing predictor by Year 5 (OR=0.771) and Year 10 (OR=0.657). Language minority barriers to regional labor market attachment do not diminish over time — they compound. By ten years out, English learner status is one of the strongest demographic predictors of sustained regional employment failure in the entire analysis.

Non-English home language graduates, who show a 42.3% no-record rate by Year 5 — the highest of any language minority subgroup — and a pattern that mirrors English learner graduates in nearly every respect. Combined with the English learner finding, these results point to a population of language minority non-completers who are not failing to find local employment — they are largely absent from the formal Minnesota economy in ways that accumulate with time.

Black non-completers, who show a distinctive dual signature that is unlike any other group. They are simultaneously overrepresented in the other-disconnected employment category — fragmented, multi-location, non-sustained employment — at rates nearly double those of White graduates at Year 1 (32.3% vs. 18.8%), and show growing no-record concentration over time. The other-disconnected rate does not resolve over five years. In the multivariate models, the Black non- completer disadvantage in sustained regional employment — less than half the odds of White graduates at every time point — is not explained by any variable in the analysis. It survives full adjustment for CTE engagement, postsecondary attendance, ACT participation, demographic controls, and local labor market conditions. It is a structural barrier of a fundamentally different character from those that educational programming can address.

American Indian non-completers, who show the highest no-record rates of any group — 44.3% by Year 5 — and the highest persistent disconnection rate (66.0%). The small group size (n=212) limits interpretive precision, but the direction is unambiguous across every analytical framework. American Indian non- completers are the most geographically absent and most persistently disconnected group in the analysis.

Non-postsecondary attenders, who show 30.5% no-record rates at Year 1 compared to 18.8% for those who attempted postsecondary — an 11.7 percentage point gap that is the largest no-record differential of any binary variable in the refined analysis. Non-attenders are not simply worse-attached locally; they are substantially more absent from the formal Minnesota labor market entirely.

AP exam participants among non-completers, who show a paradoxical geographic absence pattern — growing no-record concentration combined with growing out-of- region sustained employment — that reflects the college- orientation departure dynamic. AP test-takers who do not complete credentials are oriented away from the regional labor market in ways that persist across the full ten-year observation window.

Population 3B is the unreachable population for traditional workforce development systems. These graduates do not appear in UI wage records at rates that reflect their presence in the graduate population. Programs that use wage record identification as an entry point will systematically miss this group. Reaching them requires fundamentally different mechanisms: community-based outreach, language- accessible services, trusted intermediaries embedded in the communities where these populations live, and culturally responsive programming that does not depend on prior formal employment as a qualifying condition. For language minority populations specifically, this means programs operating through immigrant and refugee serving organizations, ESL and workforce integration programming, and employers willing to provide on-the-job language support alongside vocational training.

THE FORCES THAT SHAPE ALL THREE PATHWAYS

Beyond the pathway-specific findings, four forces operate across the full analysis in ways that shape trajectories from the point of high school graduation forward.

CTE ENGAGEMENT IS THE SINGLE MOST POWERFUL AND DURABLE PREDICTOR OF REGIONAL WORKFORCE ATTACHMENT

No other variable in the analysis — not postsecondary attendance, not academic achievement, not demographic advantage — shows as consistent, as large, and as durable an association with sustained regional employment as career and technical education depth. CTE predicts workforce-first success directly and independently. It predicts sub-baccalaureate regional completion among college completers. It channels credential completers toward regional institutions and regional employment. And its absence is the primary in-school risk factor for regional labor market disconnection. The dose- response gradient — No CTE as baseline, participants above, concentrators highest — is confirmed at every time point, in every analytical framework, across both the full population and the non-completer subgroup. At Year 10 in the non-completer multivariate model, CTE Concentrators still have 66% higher odds of sustained regional employment than No CTE graduates — a finding that has barely moved in fifteen years.

CTE is not simply a preparation program. It is the primary connective tissue between Southeast Minnesota high schools and the Southeast Minnesota regional economy. Its depth and reach in the high school experience is the single most actionable lever available to educators, workforce developers, and regional employers who want to improve the long-run regional workforce attachment of graduates.

ACADEMIC PREPARATION PREDICTS DEPARTURE, NOT REGIONAL ATTACHMENT

The college-orientation paradox is one of the most counterintuitive and analytically robust findings in the study. Among non-completers, graduates who were most academically oriented in high school — who took the ACT, who took AP exams — are consistently and independently less likely to achieve sustained regional employment. AP exam participation among non-completers is associated with 43% lower odds of sustained regional employment at Year 1, attenuating to 32% lower at Year 10 — a large and durable effect that survives complete multivariate adjustment at every time point.

This does not mean academic preparation is bad. It means that academic preparation confers labor market mobility that the Southeast Minnesota regional economy does not fully absorb. The most academically prepared non-completers are the most likely to leave — to out- of-region sustained employment, to out-of-state careers, to postsecondary attempts that do not complete but still redirect graduates away from the regional labor market. Among college completers, high academic achievement is associated with out-of-state completion and out-of-state careers. In both populations, academic orientation points away from the region.

The policy implication is not to reduce academic preparation. It is to connect academically capable graduates — completers and non-completers alike — to regional employer pathways before they exit the regional labor market. The graduates most prepared to succeed in the regional economy are the most likely to seek opportunities elsewhere, and regional workforce retention strategies that do not actively engage this population will consistently lose them.

POSTSECONDARY EXPERIENCE PROVIDES A LARGE INITIAL LIFT THAT ATTENUATES OVER TIME

Postsecondary attendance — even without credential completion — is the largest single predictor of sustained regional employment at Year 1 in the non- completer multivariate analysis (OR=2.023). By Year 10, that advantage has compressed to 28% higher odds. The attenuation is real but the association never disappears. Partial postsecondary experience provides human capital, employer networks, and labor market orientation that produce measurable benefits across the full decade. The policy case for supported postsecondary transitions — even for graduates unlikely to complete — is robust.

The contrast between postsecondary attendance and CTE engagement over time is the most analytically important temporal finding in the non-completer analysis. Postsecondary attendance provides a larger initial advantage; CTE engagement provides a more durable one. By Year 5, CTE has overtaken postsecondary attendance as the stronger predictor. By Year 10, the gap has widened further. The graduates who achieve the most durable sustained regional employment are those who combined CTE depth in high school with at least some postsecondary experience — even without a credential. These two protective factors work in the same direction and compound each other’s effects.

STRUCTURAL DISADVANTAGE OPERATES INDEPENDENTLY AND DOES NOT RESOLVE WITH TIME

The demographic disadvantages that run through every level of this analysis are not artifacts of educational preparation differences. They survive complete multivariate adjustment. Black non-completer disadvantage in sustained regional employment is essentially unchanged in the Year 10 fully adjusted model from where it was at Year 1. American Indian disadvantage is large and stable. English learner disadvantage grows stronger over the decade. Special education disadvantage attenuates but never disappears.

These are not findings that more CTE or more postsecondary exposure can fully address. They point toward structural barriers — employer discrimination, occupational segregation, geographic labor market access limitations, social network disadvantage, and the specific labor market consequences of language minority status — that operate independently of educational preparation and require responses outside the educational system. Workforce development programs that focus exclusively on preparation and training will improve outcomes for some graduates in these groups. They will not eliminate the structural gaps that persist even among the most prepared.

WHAT THE ANALYSIS MEANS FOR WORKFORCE DEVELOPMENT IN SOUTHEAST MINNESOTA

The three-pathway framework and the two-population distinction within the disconnected pathway produce a differentiated policy agenda organized around who needs what.

For the College Completer Pathway, the primary policy lever is graduation location. Getting more Southeast Minnesota graduates to complete credentials at regional institutions — particularly sub-baccalaureate technical and community college programs — is the most direct postsecondary route to regional workforce attachment. Dual enrollment programs, regional scholarship incentives, and employer partnerships that create clear credential-to-career pathways at regional institutions all work in this direction. The finding that economic and special education disadvantage actually concentrates completers at regional institutions suggests these groups, despite their lower completion rates, produce strong regional attachment when they do complete — making completion support for disadvantaged students both an equity and a regional economic priority.

For the Workforce-First Pathway, the primary policy lever is CTE depth in high school. Expanding CTE concentrator and completor programs, aligning CTE pathways with regional employer demand, and developing work-based learning and apprenticeship bridges from high school CTE into regional employment are the most direct investments in this pathway. The finding that postsecondary attendance even without completion improves outcomes suggests that short-term certificate programs, employer-sponsored training, and community college dual enrollment that provides postsecondary exposure without full credential commitment are also valuable tools for this population.

For the Locally Underutilized (Population 3A), the primary policy need is career pathway navigation and upskilling within the regional labor market. These graduates are already in the region and already employed — they need help converting marginal employment into sustained attachment. Regional workforce programs should use UI wage data to identify this population systematically — they are findable — and offer on-the-job training subsidies, industry-recognized credential attainment, career coaching, and connection to employers who can provide advancement opportunities. The growing concentration of low-income non-completers in this category over time makes early intervention before the marginal employment pattern becomes entrenched particularly important.

For the Geographically Absent (Population 3B), the primary policy need is fundamentally different — not career pathway support but basic labor market connection and outreach. These graduates cannot be reached through wage record identification. They require community-based outreach, language-accessible services, and trusted intermediaries embedded in immigrant, refugee, and minority communities. For language minority populations specifically, the finding that English learner disadvantage grows stronger over the decade — not weaker — means that early intervention before language barriers compound into permanent labor market absence is especially urgent. Employer partnerships that provide on-the- job language support alongside vocational training, and workforce programs that operate through community organizations with established trust in these communities, are the most promising approaches for this population.

CONCLUSION: WHAT THE DATA TELL US AND WHAT THEY DO NOT

This analysis tells a coherent and consequential story about the forces that shape early career trajectories in Southeast Minnesota. Where graduates went to high school within the region barely mattered. What they did in high school — how deeply they engaged with career and technical education, whether they were oriented toward college, how academically prepared they were — mattered enormously. Whether they attempted postsecondary education mattered. And the structural circumstances they carried with them — their race, their economic status, their special education classification, their language background — imposed independent barriers that preparation alone could not overcome.

The most actionable finding is also the most consistent: CTE depth is the primary lever available within the high school system for improving regional workforce attachment, and its effects are more durable than any other educational variable examined across the full decade of observation. The most urgent finding for workforce developers is the two-population structure of the disconnected pathway — locally underutilized graduates who are reachable through wage record systems and need upskilling, and geographically absent graduates who require community-based outreach and culturally responsive programming to reach at all.

What the data do not tell us is why some structural barriers persist independent of preparation — what specific employer practices, geographic constraints, or social network disadvantages produce the durable Black non-completer and English learner disadvantages that survive a decade and complete multivariate adjustment. That question requires research methods and data sources beyond the scope of this analysis. What this analysis has established is that the barriers are real, they are large, they are independent of preparation, and they do not resolve on their own. Knowing that is the necessary foundation for designing the responses that might.
