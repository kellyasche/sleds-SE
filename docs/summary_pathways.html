The pathways

The Findings: Paths Taken by High School Graduates

Overview: What Happens After High School

Tracking each graduate’s workforce and enrollment status year by year after high school reveals a gradual sorting process rather than a single fork in the road. At Year 1, the picture is still unsettled: postsecondary attendance is still absorbing a large share of the cohort, and outcomes haven’t yet separated into clear categories. By Year 5 and Year 6, sustained employment in the region climbs toward its peak — nearly 30% of graduates are working 1,000+ hours for a single employer in Southeast Minnesota by around six years out — and just over half of all graduates have meaningful employment somewhere in Minnesota by that point. At the same time, a separate and growing share of the cohort — over 30% by roughly ten years after graduation — has neither a postsecondary enrollment record nor a Minnesota employment record at all. Non-meaningful (unstable) employment holds fairly steady at around 15% throughout this window.

This pattern sets up the central structure of this report: three distinct pathways emerge from this sorting process, each with its own drivers, its own risks, and — as later sections show — its own internal complexity.

Getting to Know the Pathways

Pathway 1: The College Completers’ Pathway

Completing college is highly associated with sustained labor force participation

Overall, 46% of Southeast Minnesota graduates completed a postsecondary credential (excluding those still attending postsecondary education at the time of measurement). Credential completion is one of the strongest levers identified anywhere in this analysis: completers show substantially higher rates of sustained workforce participation than non-completers, both in the region and across Minnesota more broadly, confirming that the postsecondary pathway is the most reliable route to stable, sustained employment of any single factor examined in this project.

Who was more likely to complete a postsecondary program?

Demographic characteristics show clear, and in some cases very large, associations with who completes a credential:

  • Gender: Female graduates complete postsecondary credentials at a substantially higher rate (52.9%) than male graduates (39.5%) — a 13.4-point gap.
  • Economic status: This is the strongest demographic predictor of completion. Non-economically-disadvantaged graduates complete at 55.1%, compared to just 26.2% for economically disadvantaged graduates — a 29-point gap, the largest of any binary demographic variable.
  • Special education status: Only 14.4% of graduates with special education status complete a postsecondary credential, compared to 51.1% of graduates without — a 36.7-point gap, the single largest gap of any demographic variable examined anywhere in this analysis.
  • Race/Ethnicity: White (53.1%) and Asian/Pacific Islander (51.8%) graduates complete at the highest rates. Black (28.9%), Hispanic (26.0%), and American Indian (14.6%) graduates complete at roughly half that rate or less.

When all of these characteristics are considered simultaneously in a multivariate model, most of these gaps persist rather than washing out. Economically disadvantaged graduates and graduates with special education status show substantially lower odds of completion even after controlling for high school accomplishments; English learner status flips from a negative association at the demographic-only stage to a positive one once high school accomplishments (PSEO, ACT participation, AP exams, CTE engagement) are included — suggesting that once English learners engage with these accomplishment pathways, they complete at rates similar to or exceeding their peers, and that the raw demographic gap reflects unequal access to those accomplishments rather than a fixed disadvantage. Academic-orientation variables (ACT participation, AP exam participation, PSEO participation) are all strong positive predictors of completion, while CTE engagement shows only a modest positive relationship with completion overall — its larger role, as later sections show, is in where and how people are employed, not simply whether they complete a credential.

Economic disadvantage and special education status are the two strongest barriers to postsecondary credential completion in Southeast Minnesota, with gaps exceeding 29 and 36 percentage points respectively — the clearest, largest, and most consequential disparities found anywhere in the completion stage of this analysis.

Where Do College Completers Participate in the Labor Force?

Among people who complete a postsecondary credential, where that credential is earned turns out to be the single most powerful lever identified anywhere in this entire project. Completers who earn their credential outside the region show 69% lower odds of sustained regional employment compared to those who complete locally (OR = 0.310) — the largest effect size found in any model across the full body of work, exceeding even the effect of credential level itself.

Credential level matters too, but less than location: completers with a bachelor’s degree or higher show meaningfully lower odds of sustained regional employment than those with a sub-baccalaureate credential (associate degree or less) — a roughly 19% reduction in odds in the fully specified model (OR = 0.808), after location is accounted for. Notably, a large share of the “bachelor’s = less regional attachment” pattern seen at earlier modeling stages is actually explained by where people with bachelor’s degrees tend to complete their credentials (more often outside the region) rather than the credential level itself — location and credential level are related but distinct levers, and location is the stronger one.

What high school experiences were associated with college location?

The characteristics associated with completing college within the region and outside the region are, in several respects, mirror images of each other:

Associated with completing college in the region: - CTE engagement — both achievement level and total courses taken — is a strong, positive, and highly consistent predictor of completing a credential locally (took.ACT similarly shows a very large positive association, OR ≈ 6.4, with completing regionally, though this reflects the “engagement” side of the ACT story rather than the achievement side). - Coming from an entirely-urban high school setting and, to a lesser extent, EDR 10 specifically, both modestly increase the odds of completing locally. - Economic disadvantage is associated with somewhat higher odds of completing regionally.

Associated with completing college outside the region: - Higher ACT composite scores (among test-takers) and AP exam participation and SAT-taking are all associated with lower odds of completing locally — the clearest expression of the “two forces” pattern that recurs throughout this entire project: academic orientation and engagement pull in one direction (toward completing, and toward the region, when tied to participation), while raw academic achievement pulls toward leaving. - Higher local average wages relative to the state average are strongly associated with completing outside the region — one of the largest effects in this specific model, suggesting that in areas where local wages are more competitive, the students who leave for postsecondary education elsewhere may be disproportionately drawn by opportunities that outweigh even a locally strong wage environment, or that this reflects a different underlying selection dynamic in those communities.

Pathway 2: The Workforce-First Pathway

54% of Southeast Minnesota graduates did not complete a postsecondary credential. This is a majority of the graduating cohort — a critical framing point, since it means the “workforce-first” and “disconnected” pathways together describe the majority experience for graduates in this region, not a minority carve-out.

Understanding this group

Five years after high school, this population is genuinely split between three outcomes rather than concentrated in one: - Meaningful regional employment: 30.1% — the largest single active-workforce category by Year 5, and one that holds essentially stable through Year 10 (30.6%), suggesting this group finds its “floor” relatively early. - Meaningful MN employment (outside the region): 14.1%, rising to 16.6% by Year 10. - Non-meaningful (unstable) employment: 24.0% at Year 5, declining modestly to 18.7% by Year 10. - No MN employment record: 26.6% at Year 5, rising to 34.0% by Year 10 — becoming the single largest category of any kind for this population by ten years out.

Combined, the two “connected” workforce categories (meaningful regional + meaningful MN) account for a plurality of this population by Year 5 (44.2%) and remain the largest combined group through Year 10 (47.2%) — but the no-MN-record group’s steady growth (19.8% → 26.6% → 34.0% across Years 1, 5, and 10) is the most consequential single trend in this entire subpopulation, and is explored in depth in the Disconnected Pathway section below.

Who was more likely to stay connected to the regional workforce?

Among non-completers, several characteristics stand out as protective of sustained regional employment:

  • CTE engagement is the dominant, most consistent predictor of regional attachment in this population. Both CTE achievement level and total CTE courses taken show large, stable, positive associations with sustained regional employment across every modeling stage — the single most reliable positive lever identified for this pathway.
  • attended.ps (having attended postsecondary education without completing) is independently and substantially protective, more than doubling the odds of sustained regional employment relative to graduates who never attended postsecondary at all.
  • Gender shows a real but narrower gap in this population than in the full population: males retain a modest regional-employment advantage, but the difference is smaller than in the full cohort, since much of the full-population gender gap operated through differential postsecondary attendance and completion rates that don’t apply once completers are excluded.
  • Race/ethnicity gaps are present but comparably weaker and flatter among non-completers than in the full population, since the postsecondary sorting process that amplifies these gaps elsewhere doesn’t apply here. That said, Black non-completers show a distinct profile — concentrated in non-meaningful (unstable) employment specifically, rather than in geographic absence — that is analytically different from the American Indian and Asian/Pacific Islander profiles, where geographic absence (no MN record) is the more prominent signal.
  • ACT participation is strongly protective, while ACT composite score itself, AP exam participation, and SAT-taking are all associated with lower odds of regional attachment — the same “two forces” pattern seen throughout every stage of this project, replicated here in a population that never completed a credential at all.

Pathway 3: The Disconnected Pathway

Size of this group

Among non-completers, a substantial share falls outside of sustained employment altogether. Five years after high school, 50.6% of non-completers are either in non-meaningful (unstable) employment (24.0%) or have no Minnesota employment record at all (26.6%) — meaning roughly half of this already-majority population experiences some form of disconnection at the five-year mark. By Year 10, this combined disconnection share grows to 52.7% (18.7% non-meaningful + 34.0% no-record), driven entirely by the continued growth of the no-record category even as non-meaningful employment recedes.

Persistently Disconnected

Looking at the full five-year trajectory (not just a single point in time) sharpens this picture further. Using a persistence framework — tracking how many of the first five years after exiting formal education each person spends disconnected — 46.5% of non-completers are Persistently Disconnected (disconnected in 4 or 5 of their first 5 years), by far the largest of four categories:

Category % of Non-Completers
Consistently attached (0 years disconnected) 16.2%
Intermittently disconnected (1–2 years) 25.6%
Broadly disconnected (3 years) 11.7%
Persistently disconnected (4–5 years) 46.5%

Persistent disconnection is not a marginal, at-risk-subgroup phenomenon in this population — it is close to the single most common trajectory experienced by non-completers in Southeast Minnesota.

Two Kinds of Disconnection

A critical finding of this analysis is that “disconnection” is not one problem — it is two, and they behave very differently over time. Tracking the share of non-completers in each status across all five years after exiting formal education:

Status Year 1 Year 2 Year 3 Year 4 Year 5
Sustained workforce participation 34.6% 39.4% 42.2% 44.2% 45.9%
Non-sustained workforce participation 39.5% 33.7% 28.6% 25.6% 23.6%
No MN employment record 25.9% 27.2% 28.9% 29.5% 30.6%

Two patterns stand out. First, sustained employment and no-MN-record status both grow steadily and converge to nearly identical size by Year 5 — together accounting for over three-quarters of the population by that point. Second, and more importantly, non-sustained employment steadily shrinks over time while no-record status steadily grows — meaning the people who start out in unstable employment are gradually resolving into one of two very different end-states: either finding sustained work, or disappearing from Minnesota’s employment record entirely, rather than remaining stuck in an ongoing cycle of unstable jobs.

Separating these two mechanisms into independent persistence measures confirms they are genuinely distinct phenomena. Persistent non-sustained employment specifically (4–5 of 5 years) affects only 13.1% of non-completers, while persistent no-record status specifically affects 21.1% — neither of which, on its own, explains the 46.5% blended “persistently disconnected” figure. Decomposing the persistently-disconnected group further shows that no-record status is the dominant single driver (45.3% of that group), exceeding both non-sustained employment as the dominant driver (28.1%) and genuine switching between the two problems (26.6%). Persistent disconnection in this population is, more often than not, a single consistent problem — and more often a record-absence problem than a job-instability problem.

A Local Opportunity

Among non-completers who are working but not meeting the sustained-employment threshold (non-meaningful employment), the large majority are still employed within the region rather than elsewhere. Across all five years tracked, individuals in non-sustained employment are roughly three times as likely to be working only within Southeast Minnesota as they are to be working only outside the region, with a smaller and shrinking share showing mixed in-and-out-of-region employment patterns over time. This is a meaningful local opportunity: a substantial share of the “disconnected” population isn’t leaving the area — they’re already working locally, just below the threshold this analysis defines as sustained. Helping this specific group move from unstable to stable local employment represents a different, and in some ways more directly actionable, intervention target than reaching people who have left the region or the state’s tracked labor market entirely.

Who is Most at Risk of Disconnection?

Modeling the two disconnection mechanisms separately and simultaneously — with demographics, high school characteristics, high school accomplishments, and postsecondary attendance all considered together — reveals that different characteristics drive each mechanism, and in several cases, the same characteristic drives the two mechanisms in opposite directions.

Characteristics associated with persistent non-sustained (unstable) employment: - Special education status is the single largest and most stable predictor of persistent instability, strengthening rather than weakening as more variables are controlled (final odds ratio ≈ 1.87) — this is a genuine, durable risk, not one that gets explained away by other factors. - Black graduates show a large, stable, and non-attenuating association with persistent instability (final odds ratio ≈ 1.81) — a disparity that does not shrink no matter what other characteristics are accounted for. - Female graduates show consistently higher odds of persistent instability than male graduates. - Economic disadvantage is associated with modestly higher odds of persistent instability. - AP exam participation, counterintuitively, is associated with higher odds of instability in this specific population — likely reflecting a distinct subgroup of academically prepared students who nonetheless never completed a credential and face a different risk profile than the broader college-track population.

Characteristics associated with persistent no-MN-employment-record status: - American Indian graduates show the single largest effect found in either mechanism (odds ratio ≈ 2.23) — and, notably, show no relationship with the instability mechanism at all. Their disconnection risk is concentrated almost entirely in disappearing from the state’s tracked employment system. - English learner status is a large, stable, no-record-specific risk (odds ratio ≈ 1.67) that shows no relationship whatsoever with instability — the cleanest single-mechanism finding in this entire analysis. - Higher ACT composite scores (among test-takers) and SAT-taking are both associated with higher odds of persistent no-record status — again reflecting the “achievement pulls toward leaving” pattern that recurs throughout this project, this time in a population that never completed a credential. - Higher local average wages relative to the state average are associated with a higher risk of persistent no-record status, consistent with a mobility-related interpretation of what this status represents. - Hispanic graduates and economically disadvantaged graduates both show the reverse pattern from what appears in the instability model: each is associated with lower, not higher, odds of persistent no-record status even as they show elevated (Hispanic) or elevated (economic disadvantage) risk for instability — a genuine divergence, not simply a weaker version of the same effect.

Disparities Compound Across the Pipeline

The demographic and structural disparities identified throughout this analysis are not isolated to any one stage — they show up, and in several cases persist or widen, at every step of the pipeline: in who completes a postsecondary credential, in where completers end up working, and in who becomes persistently disconnected. Characteristics associated with not completing a postsecondary credential include:

  • Female graduates are substantially more likely to complete than male graduates — the one point in the entire pipeline where gender works in the opposite direction from most other disparities.
  • American Indian, Black, and Hispanic graduates all show significantly lower odds of completion relative to White graduates, with American Indian graduates showing the largest gap.
  • Economically disadvantaged graduates and graduates with special education status both show substantially lower odds of completion — the two largest demographic barriers to completion identified anywhere in this project.
  • English learner status, after an initial negative association, reverses to a positive association with completion once high school accomplishments are accounted for — suggesting that where English learners have equal access to PSEO, ACT participation, and similar accomplishment pathways, much of their raw completion gap closes.
  • CTE engagement and PSEO participation are both positively associated with completion, while higher ACT composite scores, AP exam participation, and SAT-taking are all positively associated with completion as well — a genuinely different pattern from every other outcome examined in this project, where these same variables typically predict lower regional attachment. Academic achievement helps you complete a credential; it just doesn’t predict that you’ll stay in the region once you do.

Because the same demographic characteristics — economic disadvantage, special education status, American Indian and Black identity — recur as barriers at the completion stage, as risk factors for one or both disconnection mechanisms among non-completers, and (for American Indian and Black graduates specifically) as one of the most persistent unexplained disparities running through the entire body of work, these disparities should be understood as compounding rather than isolated: a graduate facing one of these barriers at the completion stage is often the same graduate facing elevated disconnection risk later, through a mechanism this analysis can now specifically identify.

What Offers Protection Against Disconnection?

Protections Against Persistent Instability

The characteristics most consistently associated with lower odds of persistent non-sustained employment include:

  • Postsecondary attendance, even without completion — the single most protective factor identified against instability, reducing the odds by roughly a third.
  • CTE achievement (Concentrator/Completer level specifically) — a genuine, independent protective effect that holds even after controlling for demographics and other accomplishments.
  • took.ACT participation shows a weaker relationship with this mechanism specifically (it is a much stronger predictor of the no-record mechanism, below), but ACT participation combined with CTE engagement together represent the two accomplishment-side levers with the clearest protective relationship to job stability specifically.

Protections Against Persistent No MN Employment Record

The characteristics most consistently associated with lower odds of persistent no-record status include:

  • took.ACT participation — by a wide margin, the single strongest protective factor identified anywhere in this entire disconnection analysis, reducing the odds of persistent no-record status by roughly two-thirds. This stands in complete contrast to its near-total lack of relationship with the instability mechanism — ACT participation’s protective power is almost entirely about staying visible in Minnesota’s tracked employment system, not about job stability itself.
  • Postsecondary attendance, even without completion — again strongly protective, and here with an even larger effect than it shows against instability.
  • CTE engagement, both achievement level and total courses taken, is meaningfully protective against no-record status as well — confirming that CTE’s benefits extend to both disconnection mechanisms, though its effect on staying visible in the record is larger than its effect on job stability.
  • PSEO participation shows a modest but genuine protective effect specifically against no-record status, despite showing no relationship with the instability mechanism.

Together, these findings point to a clear and actionable takeaway: CTE engagement and any exposure to postsecondary education are the two most consistently protective levers against both forms of disconnection, while ACT participation specifically offers an outsized, specific benefit against disappearing from the state’s tracked workforce — a distinct and separately actionable lever from the broader postsecondary-access story. Interventions built around a single, undifferentiated theory of “workforce disconnection” risk missing this distinction: a program that increases CTE completion will likely help with both problems; a program that increases ACT participation will likely help disproportionately with one.
